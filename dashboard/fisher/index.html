Start here — how to read this screen

Philip Fisher was an American investor (1907–2004) whose method was qualitative before it was numerical: judge the business and the people first, the price second, and hold only while you keep checking. His framework has three layers — Quality × Price × Discipline — and this screen turns them into 6 pass/fail gates and 33 scored criteria, applied to all 64 names.

1

The Gates — six pass/fail tests, applied before any scoring 20 excluded

A gate is a veto. Fail one and the name is out, no matter how good everything else looks. These are cheap to check, so they run first.

G1Operating history — at least 3 years actually trading and 1 full year of operating profit. Excluded nobody.
G2Circle of competence — can this business be judged from documents? Fisher refused property development and anything driven by consumer fashion, because he was repeatedly wrong there. Excluded KOLTE.
G3Integrity veto — any serious question over trusteeship: a SEBI action, an auditor resignation, promoter shares pledged above 10%, an undisclosed related-party deal. Excluded 7, including CARE and NRBBEARING.
G4Disclosure symmetry — can you see how management talks in a bad quarter? Needs two consecutive periods covering an adverse event. No transcript record means you cannot know, and Fisher's rule is that unknowable means drop it. Excluded 12 — the single most destructive gate.
G5Employee treatment — no adverse labour finding. Found nothing, which is not the same as verifying good treatment.
G6Evidence sufficiency — at least two independent sources on management quality. Company self-report counts as one, however many transcripts it fills. Not applied — see the limitations panel.

What this looked like in practice: ASTEC ran an active investor-call programme through its profitable years and has published nothing in the 46 months since its losses began. HALDYN, MONARCH, AMIC and NITTA hold no calls at all. PARAS holds calls but publishes no transcripts.

2

The two mandatory business questions 11 excluded

Fisher held that two questions disqualify a company on their own, however cheap the stock is.

D3.1The summary question — "What can this company do that others could not do about as well?" A specific, evidenced answer is required. "Scale", "quality" or "relationships" with no mechanism is a fail.
D3.2Entry barriers — can newcomers start up and displace the leaders? Free entry is a fail.

Several names answered against themselves. WHEELS claims a "conversion model" as its moat while its own risk section says the business is "raw-material pass-through, not conversion." DYCL's profile says "no brand, no pricing power." DCMSHRIRAM's says "the businesses do not reinforce each other" — which is a negative answer to D3.1 by construction.

3

Dimension One — Operations 13 excluded

Seven criteria on whether the business is efficient and self-funding. This is where most of the universe died, and it died on cash rather than on price.

D1.1Cost position — operating margin at or above the median of named competitors, over 5+ years.
D1.2Shakeout test — did it stay profitable in the worst year of the cycle? Tests survival in a downturn, not whether a loss ever appeared.
D1.3Internally funded growth — three-year cash from operations covers capex plus working capital. Repeatedly issuing equity to fund operations is a fail.
D1.4Margin defence — margin held by cost engineering or mix (pass), or by price rises, duties and commodity luck (partial at best).
D1.5Technical effort — measured by output (revenue from products launched in the last 5 years), never by R&D spend.
D1.6Financial control — companies with real control do not surprise their shareholders. At most one unflagged negative surprise or restatement in 12 quarters.
D1.7Capital conservation — working-capital days stable or improving. Debtor days rising more than 30% is a fail.

The pattern: AZAD consumed ₹1,009cr of cash over three years while converting only 7% of operating profit into cash. CONCORDCS let debtor days go from 67 to 210. GARWARE's working-capital days went from 7 to 146 in a single year. None of these is visible in a P/E.

4

People, Business and Price 17 excluded

D2People (8 criteria) — the salary gradient between the top executive and the next three; whether the company depends on one person; succession from within; D2.4 the delivery scorecard, guidance promised against actual delivered; whether management admits specific mistakes; and whether it can name its most important internal long-range problem.
D3Business characteristics (10) — which moat route it uses, margin versus the closest competitor, whether it leads on profitability rather than size, recurring revenue, and D3.9 growth duration: how many years of runway are actually visible. Under 3 years is a fail.
D4Price (8) — not "is it cheap" but is there a specific belief the market holds that the evidence contradicts? If you cannot name one, the stock can never rate better than rung 2.

Note what is deliberately excluded from D4: the 52-week range, the distance from a peak, and the size of a drawdown are not inputs in either direction. Fisher: "if the fundamentals are bad enough, it may still be very high at 24."

5

The rung ladder — how a survivor is graded

Quality and price are crossed on a five-rung ladder. Quality is "HIGH" only if the company has at most ONE fail across all 25 quality criteria.

RungQualityWhat the market is payingAction
1HIGHBelow what it merits — a specific gap you can nameBuy
2HIGHRoughly in lineBuy
3HIGHAbove meritHold only — no new money
4Not highAt or below meritExcluded
5AnyFar above meritExcluded — "most dangerous group of all"

This is why two fails is fatal. Two fails means quality is not HIGH; not-HIGH can only land on rung 4 or 5; and both are excluded. A cheap price cannot rescue it, and that is the entire point of the ladder. Tier A / B / C on the survivor cards is only about position sizing — A is the most financially robust (cap 20%), B established (8–10%), C small or one-man (5%).

So when the table says "D1.7" or "Rung 5", it means: D1.7 → working capital or receivables deteriorated past the threshold. Rung 5 → priced far above what the evidence merits. Every code in the table is defined above, and the full reasoning for each name sits in GATE_PASS.md, D3_MANDATORY_PASS.md and PORTFOLIO_8.md.

The funnel

Three survivors. The criteria pre-committed to this outcome: "fewer than 8 is allowed and expected." Fisher's own funnel ran ~250 considered to 1 bought.

The rule that did the killing is arithmetic, not opinion. Quality HIGH requires ≤1 FAIL across the 25 criteria of D1–D3. The rung table then awards rungs 1–3 only to HIGH quality, and only rungs 1–2 enter a portfolio. So two FAILs anywhere is fatal regardless of price. Thirteen names died in Dimension One alone, before price was ever considered. I did not appreciate how harsh that was when writing it on 15-Aug — it stands unamended, which is the point of freezing criteria.

Other disagreements
  • The screen's top pick is MPS, which sits mid-table in the existing book.
  • The growth names all die — AZAD, NETWEB, HAPPY, UNIMECH, SANSERA — mostly on cash conversion rather than price. Fisher's Dimension One is unusually hostile to Indian capex-cycle mid-caps, and that is the biggest systematic gap between the two frameworks.

  • KOLTE was upgraded M → MH on 12-Aug; the screen excludes it at the first gate. That disagreement is about the framework's boundary, not the company.

What this screen does not claim

  • Promoter pledge was verified for 1 of 55 names. Screener does not publish it. The largest unclosed hole, and it sits inside an integrity gate.
  • D2.1, the salary gradient, was computable for only 6 of 18 finalists — and for neither surviving name. This contradicts my own pre-registration, which listed it as expected-scorable. Indian annual reports disclose ratio-to-median rather than absolute pay, and where one executive director exists the "next three" are non-executives on sitting fees. Recorded because the pre-registration exists to make this admission mandatory.
  • G6 was never applied. The obvious proxy — counting community citations — is invalid: the profile template itself scores 2, so a count of 2 measures boilerplate.
  • G5 found nothing rather than verified something.
  • Scuttlebutt does not exist here. Every management judgement rests on self-report plus documents — the exact failure Fisher named as his own largest avoidable loss.
  • Three names is a shortlist, not a portfolio. The frozen constraints need ≥5 names for tier A and ≥10 for tier B. Neither is satisfiable.
Three data traps were caught mid-run, each of which would have corrupted the result.
  • Screener's DEEP is Deep Polymers, not Deep Industries — ten years of the wrong company's P&L were parsed before the name-echo check caught it.
  • Screener prints "Transcript" in every concall row; where none exists it is an inert div with no link. Counting the text counts documents that do not exist.
  • My integrity scan had no vocabulary for the word "warning", which let CARE through the gate until a wider re-scan caught it.
  • ⤴ 8-Sep: I scored DEEP's D1.2 shakeout test by machine and got it wrong. The rule min(PAT)<0 flagged its FY25 loss — but revenue GREW 35% that year, so it was a write-off during growth, never a cyclical trough. D1.2 asks whether a company survives a downturn. Re-checked against the other D1.2 exclusion: GPIL's is correct — its loss years carried revenue of −17% and −9%, a real steel trough. DEEP stays excluded on D1.6 + D2.4 (two Q4 write-offs, the second after an explicit "nothing else left" assurance), so the verdict is unchanged and the reasoning is now right.
All three are the same failure: a plausible-looking scan pointing the wrong way.
