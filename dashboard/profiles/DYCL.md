## 1. Business in Plain English

Dynamic Cables makes the **wire that carries electricity the last few kilometres** — from the substation to the factory, the solar park, the housing block, the pumping station.

Founded in **1986 by the Mangal family** in Jaipur as a partnership, it now makes LV, MV, HV and EHV power cables, control and instrumentation cables, solar cables, railway signalling cables, building wires, aerial bunched cables and aluminium conductors.

**The analogy: DYCL is a bespoke tailor, not a garment retailer.** This is the single most important thing to understand about it, and it explains almost every number in this profile.

Polycab, KEI and RR Kabel are substantially **channel businesses** — they make standard product, push it into a dealer network, and live with the up-stocking and de-stocking that comes with it. DYCL is **100% B2B, principal-to-principal**. Every cable is cut to a customer's specification. Management were explicit when an analyst compared their volume growth unfavourably with a listed leader's:

> *"It is not an apple-to-apple comparable business with our competitors, because our competitors are more channel-driven businesses, whereas we are completely B2B business... There are lot of channel up stocking and de-stocking phenomena takes place with the increase and decrease in commodity prices in the channel business which is not applicable to us."* — Q3FY26 call

The business construct is stated by management with unusual precision, and it holds across a decade:

> **"We are typically an 80-10-10 business. So, 80% is the material cost, 10% is our employee and other expenses and 10% is our EBITDA margins, and 1% variation here and there keeps on happening."** — Q3FY26

**What this means for an investor:** DYCL is a **spread business on a commodity pass-through**, not a brand. Roughly 80% of every rupee of revenue is aluminium and PVC that flows straight through to the customer. Management say the pass-through is total and real-time:

> *"In our business, the entire price growth is escalation or de-escalation, whatever is the variation in price is passed on to our customers... Yes, 100% on a real-time basis."* — Q3FY26

About **20% of the order book is fixed-price**, and on those DYCL books the metal at order date to hedge. So the model is genuinely insulated from metal prices — but as Section 4 shows, that same pass-through is what makes DYCL's reported revenue growth a poor guide to how the business is actually doing.

**Scale:** ~₹1,285cr TTM revenue, roughly **3-4% of an estimated ₹35-40,000cr Indian B2B cable market** (management's own sizing, off a total wire-and-cable market of ~₹80,000cr). DYCL is a small fish in a very large and rapidly growing pond.

## 2. Why This Industry Exists — the durable forces

**1. India's grid is the binding constraint on everything else.** Generation capacity has run ahead of the ability to move and distribute power. Every solar park, every data centre, every new industrial estate needs cable before it needs anything else.

**2. Distribution is being rebuilt, not merely extended.** RDSS, smart metering, feeder separation and the shift of urban networks from overhead to underground all consume cable per kilometre at multiples of what the old network did.

**3. The voltage ladder is moving up.** The industry is migrating from LV toward MV/HV/EHV. This matters more than volume: higher voltage means fewer competitors, longer approval cycles and better margin. DYCL's mix has moved **HV 60% → 64% → 68%** across the last three reporting periods.

**4. Renewables changed the cable, not just the demand.** Solar plants need DC cables and specialised AC cables with different insulation and UV/temperature specs. DYCL's solar cable business went from **₹105cr (10% of FY25 revenue) to ~18% of FY26 to ~20% in Q1FY27**, and management size the addressable solar-cable market at **"₹5,000 crores upwards"** against their own ₹200-250cr.

**5. Approvals are the real barrier, and they take years.** To supply a discom or a PGCIL-grade project you need type-tested, approved product. Management returned to this repeatedly when asked about Adani and Birla entering cables:

> *"It's a long approval process which one has to follow if you have to supply cables and we are — there are the kind of approvals which we carry only few players in the country have and that is what I believe is a long journey for any new entrant."* — Q1FY27

## 3. What Makes This Company Different — the moat

**1. A genuinely B2B DNA, deliberately maintained.** DYCL has repeatedly refused to chase the retail/B2C wire market even as peers built brands there. On entering building wire:

> *"There is no ways we are getting into the distribution or branding or all these activities for the Building Wire segment... we don't want to get into that competitive and low margin space at all because that's never been the philosophy of the company."* — Q1FY27

**2. Approval stock.** UL certification, **PGCIL approval**, NABL-accredited in-house laboratory, AL59 conductor approval, and BIS. These are multi-year assets that don't appear on the balance sheet.

**3. Willingness to walk away from bad revenue.** This is the most under-appreciated thing about DYCL and it is *visible in the numbers*. Management exited two segments on margin grounds:
- **Railway signalling cables:** 4% of FY25 revenue → **zero in FY26**. *"Competitive intensity is very high and it is better to move on to our power cable products where... margin profile is much better."*
- **Low-voltage conductors:** 8-9% → **sub-5%**, a 50-60% volume decline, deliberately.

Very few sub-₹2,500cr companies voluntarily shrink ~12% of their revenue base to protect margin. It is the clearest evidence in this file that the 10-11% EBITDA band is managed, not lucky.

**4. Aluminium-led, not copper-led.** *"95% is aluminium for us."* This is a real differentiator: DYCL's working capital per rupee of revenue is structurally lighter than a copper-heavy peer's, and it sits naturally in the T&D/conductor space rather than the house-wire space.

**5. Fungible capacity.** Management stress that plant and machinery can be redeployed across product families — which is why exiting railway signalling cost them no stranded assets, and why HTLS conductors and data-centre cables can be addressed without dedicated capex: *"our machineries are fungible enough."*

**6. Elite capital efficiency for the sector.** **ROCE 26% for two consecutive years; ROE 20% on a 3-, 5- and 10-year basis.** Borrowings down from ₹119cr (FY24) to **₹43cr (FY26)**. This is a low-debt, high-return business, and that is not typical of small cable makers.

**7. A 10-year record that is genuinely good.** Sales CAGR 16% / profit CAGR 28% over 10 years; 28% / 53% over 5 years.

### Where the moat is weaker — stated plainly

- **3-4% market share against giants who are all expanding.** Polycab, KEI, RR Kabel, Havells — plus **Adani and Birla now entering**. Management's answer on Adani/Birla was candid but thin: *"I don't know what is their business plan... we still need to wait and watch."*
- **No brand, no pricing power.** In a pure B2B tender market, *"you have to compete on the lowest price."* Management's own words. The 10-11% margin band is a ceiling as much as a floor.
- **Customer concentration is undisclosed.** DYCL does not break out top-customer share. This is a real gap.
- **The moat is approvals + discipline, not technology.** Nothing here is patented.

### 🔴 THE MOST IMPORTANT NUMBER IN THIS PROFILE

**Revenue grew 33%. Volume grew 5-6%.**

> *"If we break out that volume and value growth, **5% to 6% is our volume growth** and balance is large part of it is kind of derived by the price growth of aluminium and largely aluminium."* — Q1FY27 call

Aluminium prices spiked sharply from March 2026. Because DYCL passes metal cost through 1:1, **a metal spike mechanically inflates revenue without adding a rupee of real activity** — and equally, a metal collapse would deflate it. The 33% is roughly **27 points of aluminium and 6 points of business.**

This is not a criticism of the company; it is a warning about the screen. **Anyone valuing DYCL off headline revenue growth is valuing aluminium.**

The volume series across the last four reported periods:

| Period | Reported revenue growth | **Underlying volume growth** |
|---|---|---|
| 9MFY26 | +21% | **+17%** |
| Q3FY26 alone | +19% | **+2-3%** |
| Q4FY26 alone | +7% (core +20%) | **+8%** (core products) |
| **Q1FY27** | **+33%** | **+5-6%** |

Management's explanations for the weak volume quarters are coherent and partly verifiable: a high base (*"last Q1 our volume growth was around 25%, 26%"*), the deliberate exit from conductors/railway cables depressing reported volume (conductors are pure metal, so they dominate the volume metric — *"this quarter it was 4.5%, and last year it would be around 9%"*), and genuine order deferral as customers balked at elevated aluminium prices:

> *"**Price is king.** So, I mean nobody is willing to book material at such high prices for project executions in Q3. Nobody wants to do it."* — Q1FY27

But even granting all of that, **the two-year volume trend is mid-single-digit, against an 18-20% value growth guidance.** That gap is the central analytical question on this stock.

## 5. Connecting the Dots

The threads reinforce each other **only if the plant lands.**

**The virtuous version:** approvals stock (PGCIL, UL, AL59, NABL) → access to higher-voltage, higher-margin work → mix shifts HV 60%→68% → margin drifts 10.3%→10.9% → cash pays down debt (₹119cr→₹43cr) → credit rating improves → interest halves (₹21cr→₹11cr) → PAT compounds faster than revenue (FY26 PAT +29% on revenue +17%). **This chain is real and it already happened.** It is why ROE is 20% and ROCE 26%.

**Where the chain breaks:** every link above is about *profitability per unit*, and DYCL has now run out of *units*. At 85% utilisation the margin engine has nothing left to work on. The new plant is not incremental nicety — it is the only thing that converts a good margin story into a growth story. And it is 15-18 months late.

**The second-order effect nobody has priced:** the same plant carries **E-beam** and **DC solar cable** capability. Solar is already ~20% of revenue growing 25-30%, and DYCL currently supplies only the AC half of a solar plant's cable requirement. DC capability roughly doubles its wallet share per solar project. **That is the highest-confidence growth lever in this file** — higher than US exports, far higher than HTLS or data centres.

**The optionality, ranked by how much I'd credit it:**
1. **DC solar cables** — real, funded, arriving Sept-2026. Credit it.
2. **US exports** — real, first shipments made, but tiny (15% of a 13% export share) and distribution-led with no committed volume. Small credit.
3. **HTLS conductors (TS Conductor Corp tie-up)** — genuine technology access, few Indian players have it, capacity is fungible. But *"there is no hard timeline"*, type-testing takes ~a year, and adoption depends on utilities re-engineering their cost base. **Option, not a forecast.**
4. **Data-centre power cables** — management were commendably honest: *"it is in a very, very nascent stage as of now in our country"*, timelines *"really tricky"*. **Credit zero for now.**
5. **B2B building wire** — new, deliberately un-branded, margin unproven.

## 7. What Could Prove Us Wrong

1. **🔴 Volume growth never recovers.** The single biggest risk. If 5-6% is the real run-rate rather than a base/deferral artefact, then the 18-20% guidance is an aluminium illusion and the stock is not cheap at 24.7x — it is fairly priced for a 6% grower.
2. **🔴 Aluminium reverses.** Revenue falls mechanically. Management have already noted *"we have already seen a reversal in raw material prices."* Margin is protected; the optics are not.
3. **🔴 The plant slips a ninth time.** Eight consecutive misses is a pattern, not bad luck. Every quarter of delay is a quarter of capacity rationing with the order book already flat.
4. **🔴 The ₹344.75cr related-party guarantee question is unresolved.** Against ₹457cr of net worth. Until the FY26 AR is read, this is an unquantified governance exposure. **Not an allegation — an open item.**
5. **🟡 Adani and Birla enter B2B power cables in earnest.** Management's answer amounted to *"we'll wait and watch."* Approval cycles protect DYCL for perhaps 2-3 years, not forever.
6. **🟡 Working capital keeps absorbing the cash.** WC days 56→74→82; FY26 FCF ₹4cr. Growth from here needs either working capital release or fresh borrowing.
7. **🟡 Key-man and family-governance concentration.** One MD carries the operating story; five family members in senior roles; two independent directors.
8. **🟡 Customer concentration is undisclosed.** Cannot be assessed. That is itself a finding.
9. **🟡 Institutional ownership of 1.65% means no external scrutiny** and severe liquidity risk on any exit.
10. **🟢 US tariff policy reverses again.** Already cost DYCL ~18 months once. The entry is real but fragile.

**Dynamic Cables is Ajinkya Rahane.**

Technically correct, quietly effective, and consistently underrated next to flashier names in the same dressing room. **Thesis in a sentence:** *A genuinely well-run, debt-light, high-ROCE B2B cable maker trading at less than half its peers' multiple, where the entire case rests on one 15-months-late plant finally commissioning in September 2026 and converting a proven margin story into an actual volume story — with the honest caveat that today's headline growth is aluminium, not activity.*

**Conviction: MEDIUM.**
