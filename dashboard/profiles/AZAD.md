## 1. Business in Plain English

Azad makes **very small, very precise pieces of metal that go inside things that must not fail** — gas turbines that generate electricity, jet engines that fly aircraft, nuclear-plant turbines, oil-and-gas control systems, mission-critical defence hardware.

When Mitsubishi Heavy Industries builds a gas turbine, the combustion section contains **nozzle vanes** — small aerofoil blades that channel hot gas onto the rotating blades. **If one cracks mid-operation, the turbine shreds. Only three companies in the world can make that part reliably. Azad is one.**

**The transaction is unusual for how patient it is.** Azad does not sell components off a shelf. A prospective customer — **GE, Siemens, MHI, Baker Hughes, Rolls-Royce, EDF-Arabelle for nuclear** — first *qualifies* Azad over **five to ten years**: process audits, metallurgical testing, first-article inspections, serial-production trials, delta-qualifications per part number. Once through, you sign a **five-to-eight-year purchase agreement**, and Azad manufactures at a **dedicated facility built specifically for that customer.** Then you repeat with the next one.

**At scale Azad compounds one specific asset: qualified part numbers × qualified customers × wallet share on each. Today: 1,700+ qualified parts across 45+ specialised manufacturing processes. That number is the moat.**

**The snapshot:**
- **Hyderabad. Founded 2008 by Rakesh Chopdar** — twelve years of grind before the first meaningful global qualification.
- **FY26 revenue ₹603cr (+32%), EBITDA margin 37.4%, PAT ₹134cr consolidated at a 22.4% PAT margin** — on an **effective tax rate of 36%**, high because overseas entities carry more tax and deferred tax on German losses cannot currently be recognised.
- **Q1FY27 standalone: revenue ₹170.5cr (+26.8%), EBITDA ₹64cr (+32.1%) at a 37.6% margin**, up 150bps. Consolidated revenue ₹172.6cr, PAT ₹35.2cr.
- **🟢🟢 Delivered India's first indigenous expendable turbojet engine** to GTRE/DRDO/MoD — manufactured, assembled and integrated **entirely in-house**.
- **Four dedicated OEM plants live** (Mitsubishi, GE Power Systems, Siemens Energy, Baker Hughes); **eight planned.**
- **🔴 FY26 operating cash flow −₹119cr; free cash flow −₹691cr; cash conversion cycle 1,720 days.**

**Big picture in one sentence:** Azad is a genuinely rare precision-manufacturing franchise — dedicated plants for four global OEMs, 1,700+ qualified parts, 37% margins, and now an **engine-integration credential only a handful of countries hold** — trading at **126x on 11.9% ROCE with negative operating cash flow**, where the base case loses money even on 30% compound earnings growth.

## 2. Why This Industry Exists

**Global aerospace and energy OEMs face an impossible trade-off.** Their end products — a jet engine running 25,000 hours between overhauls, a gas turbine producing power for 30 years — depend on components that must meet **"zero PPM"** defect rates. They could make them in-house, but that means owning specialised heat-treatment lines, vacuum furnaces, five-axis machining centres and PhD metallurgists for every product line — uneconomic below very high volumes.

**So OEMs consolidate to two or three qualified vendors per critical component and lock them in**, because switching is a two-year exercise.

**The resulting economics are unusual:**
- **High margins** — 35–40% EBITDA is normal at the supplier level, because customers pay for reliability, not price
- **Long sales cycles** — 5–10 years from first RFP to first purchase order
- **Very sticky revenue** — contracts run 5–8 years and typically renew
- **Enormous capital intensity** — a dedicated, specialised facility per major customer

**The global supplier universe is tiny.** For hot-section rotating parts, aerospace airfoils and nuclear-grade rotor components you can count the qualified players on two hands: **Howmet Aerospace (US), Precision Castparts (US, Berkshire), Doncasters (UK)**, a few Japanese and German specialists — **and Azad.**

**The demand backdrop is arguably the strongest in twenty years.** Global aircraft backlog exceeds **15,000 units**; power-generation OEMs are running full order books on **AI-datacentre electricity demand**; **nuclear is reviving** after fifteen years of neglect; **defence budgets are rising globally.** And because qualification cycles are so long, **new entrants cannot step in on a five-year view.**

## 3. What Makes This Company Different

**The moat is real, structural and named: customer-locked-in qualification status.** Every dedicated facility — **GE, Siemens, MHI, Baker Hughes**, with four more planned — represents a customer who has audited the plant, approved the processes and effectively made Azad an inside supplier for that programme's life. **Ripping that out is a two-year, seven-figure decision no OEM makes lightly.**

Chopdar on why the structure matters: *"Operating dedicated plants represent the **deepest possible level of customer integration** in our industry. It creates immense operational stickiness and provides long-term multiyear supply chain visibility with global marquee OEMs."*

**The MHI single-source award for hot-section nozzle vanes is the strongest possible endorsement.** Vishnu Malpani: *"there are only 3 players around the world of precision manufacturing that have been able to crack this materially."* **MHI could have used their in-house German or Japanese supplier and chose Azad after multi-year audits. That is a datum, not marketing.** The **MHI hot-section contract has since closed** — a materially larger TAM than cold section — and *"other OEMs have shown super interest and asking us when is your facility ready."*

**Three specifics reinforce it:**
- **Backward integration** — in-house **heat treatment (NADCAP approved, 2026)**, vacuum heat treatment, shot peening and coatings. Fewer outsourced processes, tighter quality control, higher margins.
- **Zero-PPM culture** — mid-cap Indian precision manufacturers typically run **500–1,000 PPM**; aerospace and turbine work demands **single digits**. The organisational discipline behind that took two decades to build.
- **Founder-CEO Rakesh Chopdar** — founded Azad in 2008 and spent **twelve years** earning the first meaningful global qualification (Energy reached ₹120cr by 2020). The 5x growth of the last five or six years came *after* that grind. **This is not an overnight success dressed up as one**, and it is why an order book at 11–12x revenue is credible.

**🟢🟢 And now: an engine-integration credential.** Azad **manufactured, assembled and delivered India's first indigenous expendable turbojet engine** to GTRE/DRDO/MoD. Chopdar: *"this milestone is a **profound architectural shift. We have successfully evolved from being a precision component manufacturer into a fully integrated propulsion system player.**"* Asked directly whether metallurgy and surface treatment were outsourced: *"**we have done everything in-house. There's nothing that we have outsourced.**"* **And it needs no new capex at initial volumes** — *"all the capacity what we have are fungible."*

**🆕 Raw-material localisation is a genuine margin lever.** Material was *"more or less imported"*; **Sunflag and Star Wire are now qualified domestically for critical grades** — and Azad *"helped them to get qualified with the giants like GE and the Siemens, not for India, but for the **global supply chain**."* Worth a price benefit plus **4–5% of transportation cost**, and already visible in the 150bps EBITDA expansion.

**Where the moat is weaker — and this half is growing:**
- **🔴 Cash conversion is the weakest in this watchlist.** FY26 CFO **−₹119cr** on ₹384cr of operating profit (**−31%**); FCF **−₹691cr**; **cumulative FY22–26 FCF −₹1,199cr**.
- **🔴 Inventory days doubled to 2,067; the cash conversion cycle went 804 → 1,720 days — 4.7 years.**
- **🔴 The order book was not quantified in Q1FY27** — the first time since listing — and was **flat at ~₹6,500cr between Feb-26 and May-26.**
- **Founder key-person dependency.** Chopdar is the face and much of the reason for the moat.
- **Wallet-share saturation risk** — OEM procurement policies often cap any single supplier around 40%.
- **An asset base built for far more than current volumes:** roughly **₹1,500cr gross block and ~₹1,200cr net block including CWIP by FY27-end** against FY26 revenue of ~₹604cr. **That is the bull case and the risk in one sentence.**

### The CFO's answer — candid, and worth reading closely

> **Amit Dixit (Goldman Sachs):** *"in Q1 FY27, we saw that there was a bit of increase in finance costs… this working capital in terms of days, when we can expect it to plateau?"*
> **Ronak Jajoo (CFO):** *"finance cost will going to continue because you have a **bill discounting charges**, which will go into the finance cost only. So, there is a **betterment of the cash flow — operating cash flow, not on the financial cost side**… **we are targeting H1 around 200 days. And by H2, we are targeting in the range of 160 to 180** type of days. So this is largely back on the **bill discounting facility where our current debtor stage, which are around 170, 180 days will go down to 90 days**."*

**Give management full credit for the honesty of that answer** — the CFO said plainly that the improvement appears in *operating* cash flow while the cost sits in *finance* costs. **But be clear what it means: discounting a bill converts a receivable into cash today at a price.** **Reported debtor days fall from ~180 to 90 and CFO improves without a single customer paying sooner.** The economics move from the working-capital line to the interest line — **which is precisely why finance costs rose 67% year-on-year and why management says they *"will going to continue."***

> **My read, labelled as mine:** the H2 targets are achievable **because they are financeable**, not because the cash cycle is shortening. **The number to watch is not working-capital days — it is whether FY27 operating cash flow turns positive without discounting flattering it, and what the finance cost does to PAT while it happens.**

## 5. Connecting the Dots

**The Azad flywheel is simple but slow-moving:**

**Step 1 — earn a qualification.** The client audits Azad for two to five years. Cost to Azad: **₹5–15cr per qualification**. Revenue during qualification: minimal.
**Step 2 — win the first PO.** Revenue small for two years; the client gives 5–10% wallet share and capital sits underutilised.
**Step 3 — build a dedicated facility.** **₹50–100cr** invested. Revenue flows steadily; wallet share climbs to 30–50%.
**Step 4 — earn adjacent qualifications with the same customer.** *This is where the compounding starts* — subsequent part numbers run through much shorter cycles, and wallet share can reach 60–80% on a product family.
**Step 5 — horizontal transfer.** Capabilities built for GE become sellable to Siemens, then Baker Hughes, then MHI, then Pratt & Whitney. Malpani: *"the capabilities that Azad is building across our customers are very selectively building it so that **we can horizontally deploy it** and grow the business in the years to come."*

**It is the capability portfolio that compounds, not the customer count.** And the turbojet is that logic taken one level up: **an entire engine, integrated in-house, on fungible existing capacity** — moving Azad from part supplier to system player without a new plant.

**Where the compounding breaks: capacity ramp, not demand.** Management has been explicit that **25% growth is the constrained number, not the demand number.** Goldman's Amit Dixit pushed back that 25% looked low given OEM backlogs and Chopdar conceded *"you're right."* **If the H2FY27 ramp slips, growth falls to 15–18% and the multiple has nowhere to stand.**

**And the tension the flywheel cannot resolve on its own is cash.** Each step consumes capital years before it returns any — qualification, then dedicated plant, then inventory built ahead of a ramp. **That is structurally why operating cash flow has been positive once in five years.** The question is not whether the model consumes cash — it must — **but whether it stops consuming it once the eight plants are built and utilised.** FY27 is the first year that becomes testable, and the stated remedy is bill discounting rather than collection.

## 6. Why the Market Is Paying This Multiple

**At ₹2,718: 126x trailing, 11.5x book, on 11.9% ROCE and 9.09% ROE, with no dividend, at an all-time high.**

**The market is paying for four specific things, and three of them are real:**
1. **Revenue visibility that is genuinely rare.** ₹6,500cr of order book against ~₹604cr of revenue is **11–12x book-to-bill.** Very few Indian companies have this. **It deserves a premium — but not any premium**, and the book has now been flat for two quarters and undisclosed for one.
2. **Global-peer margins on an Indian cost base.** **Howmet Aerospace trades near 30x forward on 25% EBITDA margins. Azad runs 37%.** Part of the multiple is that the margin structure is world-class while the cost base is not.
3. **Founder plus moat.** A first-generation founder who built a global-quality organisation over eighteen years. **Founder-execution premium is a real thing in Indian mid-caps.**
**What gives me pause in what is priced in:** the market is assuming margin expansion continues **past 38%** when management guides **33–35%** — **if margins settle at 33%, FY27 PAT is closer to ₹165cr than ₹190cr.** And the multiple assumes the capability-transfer story keeps working: **true so far, not guaranteed as customer concentration shifts.**

4. **Four simultaneous sector tailwinds** — AI-datacentre electricity demand, a 15,000-unit aircraft backlog, nuclear revival, global defence rebuild. **All four are real and multi-year.**

**Against Indian peers:** **Data Patterns ~70x, MTAR Tech ~55x, Bharat Electronics ~40x. Azad sits at the very top of a premium sector** — earned by growth rate and order-book visibility, **but leaving no room for execution error.**

**Base numbers:** FY26 revenue ~₹604cr, PAT ~₹134cr; TTM ₹640cr / ₹140cr. On >25% guided growth, FY27 revenue ~₹760cr and PAT ~₹160–165cr (EPS ~₹25) → **~108x FY27E.**

## 7. What Could Prove Us Wrong

**Risk 1 — 🔴 Valuation.** 126x trailing, ~108x forward, 11.5x book, no dividend, at an all-time high. **The base case requires a de-rating to 70x and still loses money.**

**Risk 2 — 🔴 Operating cash flow.** Positive once in five years; FY26 **−₹119cr**, CFO/OP **−31%**, FCF **−₹691cr**, cumulative five-year FCF **−₹1,199cr**. **Watch:** whether H1FY27 turns, and how much of any improvement is bill discounting.

**Risk 3 — 🔴 The cash conversion cycle doubled to 1,720 days** and inventory days to 2,067. **Does the H2 ramp consume the build, or add to it?**

**Risk 4 — 🔴 Capacity ramp-up slips.** *The top-ranked operating risk.* Four facilities in ramp now, four more to build in FY27. **Managing quality across eight dedicated OEM facilities simultaneously is operationally demanding, and a single quality finding at any one can pause order flow while a customer audits.**

**Risk 5 — 🟡 The unexplained tax rate.** 30% → 10%, unaddressed by management and unasked by analysts, and the entire reason PAT rose while PBT fell.

**Risk 6 — 🟡 The order book: flat, then undisclosed.** ₹6,500cr+ in Feb, ~₹6,500cr in May, no figure in August, for a company whose case rests on multi-year visibility.

**Risk 7 — 🟡 Founder key-person dependency.** Chopdar is the face and much of the reason for the moat. **Any founder-transition event is a real risk.**

**Risk 8 — 🟡 Wallet-share saturation.** The horizontal-transfer story assumes customers keep granting more part-number qualifications. **If a major OEM caps any single supplier at ~40% wallet share — a real procurement policy — the growth ramp tops out.**

**Risk 9 — 🟡 Rolls-Royce, Pratt & Whitney and Safran qualifications.** The next legs of aerospace growth. **If they do not convert from qualification to purchase orders on the guided timeline (Q4FY27 for Rolls-Royce, FY28 for the others), the aerospace trajectory misses.**

**Risk 10 — 🟡 A further capital raise.** Promoter holding has been flat at 55.84% for four quarters after a 10-point dilution; the asset base is still growing and cash flow is negative. **If working capital does not normalise through FY28, the balance sheet needs equity — and that changes the story.**

**Risk 11 — 🟡 Institutions selling into retail buying**, 2.84 points in a single quarter at an all-time high.

**Risk 12 — ⚪ Global aerospace slowdown.** Boeing production cuts or an engine-OEM halt; Airbus and Boeing programmes are 30–40% of aerospace revenue.

**Risk 13 — ⚪ Middle East geopolitics.** The Baker Hughes Saudi facility was already flagged as *"shifted timelines"* on regional priorities.

**Risk 14 — ⚪ Energy-transition surprise.** Faster displacement of gas turbines by renewables would compress the ~81.5%-of-revenue Energy segment. **Low probability near-term, real 2030+ risk.**

**Risk 15 — ⚪ Currency.** Significant USD-denominated revenue; **5%+ rupee appreciation would compress margins 100–150bps** — though management notes it flows below EBITDA and imports offset part of it.
