Why a machine-tool builder lives or dies on its shelf

An industry primer for Macpower CNC Machines — what a CNC machine tool physically is, why assembling one takes days while sourcing its parts takes six months, and why that single asymmetry decides the inventory, the moat, the margin and the cash
Built 11-Sep-2026 · Sourced from Macpower's own transcripts, exchange filings and the FY26 Annual Report on disk · Companion to Company Profiles/MACPOWER.md

This page explains the industry, not the investment case. There is no valuation here, no conviction and no price target — those live in the profile. What follows is the mechanism: what the machine is, who buys it and how they pay for it, why a builder of 364 variants has to own nineteen thousand different components before a customer has ordered anything, and how that choice shows up in three separate places in the accounts where a reader who does not know the business will misread it.

1. What a CNC machine tool physically is

Start with the object, because nothing else makes sense until you can see it.

A CNC machine tool is a machine that removes metal to a drawing. You clamp a piece of steel or aluminium into it, a computer reads a program, and motors drive a hardened cutting tool against the metal along precisely controlled paths until what is left is the part you wanted. "CNC" is Computer Numerical Control — the computer is what separates it from a manual lathe, where a machinist turns handwheels by eye.

It is a capital good, not a component. It is bought once by a factory, sits on that factory's floor for fifteen or twenty years, and produces parts for the whole of that life. That distinction decides everything about the industry: who buys it, how they pay for it, how often they buy again, and why the seller's order book looks nothing like a component supplier's.

2. Turning and milling — the two motions that define the product range

Almost every metal-cutting machine is one of two things, and the difference is simply what spins.

Combining them is where the money is. A part that needs turning and milling can be made on two cheap machines, with a human moving it between them and introducing an error at every transfer — or on one turn-mill centre in a single clamping. The second is faster, more accurate and more expensive, and that upgrade path is what the industry means by "moving up the value chain."

The one number that frames the whole catalogue

Macpower offers 364 variants (Q1FY27 call; the Q4FY26 call says "more than 400 variants" — the two figures are used loosely and interchangeably by management). A variant is a distinct configuration: bed size, spindle power, turret type, control brand, bar capacity, automation. Each machine contains roughly 990 components (Q4FY26 call; "1,000 approximate" on Q1FY27).

Hold those two numbers together and the operating problem appears on its own: you cannot know which of 364 configurations a customer will ask for next, but whichever it is, you need a specific subset of ~990 parts on hand to build it. Section 5 is about what that costs.

3. Where the machine sits in the chain

Macpower is not in the automotive supply chain. It sells to it. The distinction matters because it inverts who the customer is and how the demand behaves.

Two consequences follow, and both run counter to intuition.

First, the customer is small and credit-constrained. A tier-3 job shop buying a ₹20 lakh machine typically finances it with a bank loan under ₹1 crore and a state subsidy. The CMD is explicit that this is the segment Macpower is built around and that corporates behave differently: "Now corporates do not have the requirement of a bank facility. Our types of customers are the ones who take loans below INR 1 crore, so they get a subsidy." (Q3FY25 call). That bank-and-subsidy dependency is the single biggest determinant of when revenue lands, and it reappears in Section 6.

Second, demand is broad rather than deep, and the company has deliberately declined to segment it. Asked repeatedly to break the order book down by sector, the CMD refuses on cost grounds: "wherever you put your hand, there is an order… if I work on segment-wise and sector-wise for 10%, then the cost of manpower, offices, and branches cost will increase." (Q1FY27 call). With roughly 2% of Indian machine-tool production, the argument is that specialising is a cost you take on only when the easy demand runs out. MY INFERENCE That is a coherent strategy and also a real analytical limitation — there is no sector mix disclosed anywhere, so an outsider cannot test how cyclical the book is.

4. The bill of materials — what is bought and what is made

Of the ~990 components in a machine, Macpower makes some and buys the rest. The split is disclosed, and it comes with a caveat that is easy to miss and changes what the number means.

Why "40–45% backward integrated" is a weaker statement than it sounds

It counts parts, not rupees. A machine's ~990 components include hundreds of brackets, fasteners, covers and fittings that are cheap and easy to make, and a handful of items — the control above all — that are expensive and impossible to make. Making 45% of the items is consistent with making a much smaller share of the cost.

MY INFERENCE This is why backward integration delivers margin slowly rather than in a step. It is also why the 20% that is permanently bought is the ceiling on the whole strategy: the most valuable single component in the machine sits inside it, and no amount of new plant changes that.

5. The mechanism: six months to source, days to assemble

This is the fact the whole business is organised around, and once you have it, the inventory, the moat, the margin and the cash all follow without further explanation.

Assembling a CNC machine is fast. Obtaining its parts is slow. Not slightly slow — an order of magnitude slow, and for the critical imported items, slow in a way no amount of factory efficiency can compress.

"If I import something from Taiwan, then their delivery time is three months, and logistic time is now two months. So, I will not get it before six months. … the assembly time is not much different, but the time of raw material and process, lead time, logistic time, and process time, that is too much."
— Rupesh Mehta, CMD, Q4FY26 earnings call, 28-May-2026

What that buys, and what it costs

Holding the shelf is a choice with a clear payoff and a clear price, and management argues the payoff openly:

"We have 364 variants, now 70% business, which will be executed, month by month, can be predicted. 30% is unpredictable. So which variant, which model you will get, you don't know. So if you have inventory, then you can execute… Today, in my one machine there are 1,000 approximate components. I manage 19,000 plus inventory. If one component is not there, then this 364 variant, one or two machines, you will not be able to dispatch."
— Q1FY27 earnings call, 30-Jul-2026

There are three secondary benefits management names, and they are real: bulk purchase discounts, full-container logistics economics, and insulation from an input-cost spike — "many our peers companies are struggling in the import component, especially ball screw, LM guideways, rotary tables, higher end encoder… I have an inventory of one year for import, today, so that threat is not there" (Q4FY26 call). It also makes a war or a shipping disruption a non-event in the short run: asked about West Asia, the CMD's answer was that a year of cover means "it's a 0% effect."

And the price of it, stated plainly

The shelf has to be bought before it can be used, and it has to grow every time the business grows. In FY26 inventory rose ₹108.79cr → ₹145.55cr — an absorption of ₹36.76cr, more than the entire year's profit after tax of ₹33.87cr (FY26 Annual Report, note 7 and cash flow statement). Section 8 traces exactly where that left the cash.

There is one genuine structural risk the shelf creates rather than solves. Ball screws and LM guideways come from a single company in Taiwan — "only ball screw and LM guideways by one company" (Q1FY27 call). A year of stock converts a supply shock into a twelve-month problem instead of an immediate one. It does not remove it.

6. How an order is born, and why finished machines wait

The shelf explains how fast Macpower can build. It does not explain when Macpower gets paid, or when revenue appears. That is a separate sequence, and it has a step in it that surprises people.

Step 4 is the unusual one. Most manufacturers ship and then chase payment. Macpower does the reverse:

"as long as I am taking 100% money in the market, I do not give the machine without advance, or I do not even make it."
— Q1FY27 earnings call

That is why trade receivables are small relative to revenue and are described as PSU and bank letters settling in days rather than trade credit. It also means the credit risk in this business does not sit in receivables. It sits in inventory — Macpower's capital is at risk in components it has bought and machines it has built, not in money customers owe.

Step 7 is the one that shows up in the accounts. A machine can be finished, tested and paid for, and still sit on the floor because the customer's loan sanction or subsidy paperwork has not cleared. Management describes this as permanent and routine, not exceptional:

"Every month, some of the machine is forwarded on month-on-month basis and quarter-on-quarter basis… Even now, we have the same numbers forwarded for next quarter. This is the journey."
— Q1FY27 earnings call

In FY26 that pile was unusually large at the year-end: finished goods inventory rose from ₹1.33cr to ₹12.89cr, close to a ten-fold increase (FY26 Annual Report, note 7). An investor put the resulting question to management directly on the Q1FY27 call — whether the roughly 80 completed machines waiting at March-end had converted — and was told about 90% had. MY INFERENCE At the ~₹20 lakh average realisation, ₹12.89cr of finished goods carried at cost implies something in the region of 60 to 100 machines, which is the right order of magnitude for the "80 machines" figure discussed on the call. The machine count is arithmetic, not disclosure — Macpower does not publish unit volumes.

7. Reading the numbers I — you cannot annualise a quarter

Machine tools are a capital good bought on a bank loan by a small business, and small businesses buy capital goods at the end of the financial year. The consequence is a seasonal pattern so pronounced that multiplying any quarter by four gives the wrong answer — and in Macpower's case, wrong by more than a third.

Management describes the shape consistently, and has done so for two years: "the first quarter is 15%, 20% of the business, because there is no balance in March, there is no financing… quarter 2 and quarter 3, it is almost similar. And whatever is left in quarter 4, it is 30% to 35%." (Q1FY27 call; the same split is given on the Q1FY26 call). The reasons are mundane and structural: customers time purchases to their own year-end depreciation and subsidy cycles, bank sanctions cluster before March, and the IMTEX trade exhibition falls in January.

What that does to a naive reading of Q1FY27

Q1FY27 revenue was ₹95.24cr. Here is what four different methods make of it.

The general rule for this industry

In a capital-goods business with an end-of-year buying pattern, a quarter is only comparable to the same quarter of the prior year, never to the quarter before it and never to one-fourth of a year. Macpower's Q1FY27 revenue of ₹95.24cr was below the preceding Q4's ₹100.29cr, which looks like a sequential decline and is nothing of the sort — it is a seasonally weak quarter running at 95% of a seasonally strong one.

8. Reading the numbers II — where ₹100 of operating profit goes

A machine-tool builder that is growing converts a strikingly small fraction of its profit into cash, and the reason is not inefficiency. It is the shelf from Section 5, which has to be enlarged every time revenue rises.

Why the conventional working-capital metrics mislead here

Look at Macpower's working-capital ratios in isolation and they read like a company losing control of its receivables. They are not. Almost the entire deterioration is inventory, and receivables barely participate.

A conventional manufacturer's risk runs: buy → make → sell → wait to be paid. The exposure is a customer who may not pay, and the metric that matters is debtor days. Macpower's runs: buy six months early → get paid → make → dispatch. The exposure is components bought for a machine nobody has ordered yet, and the metric that matters is inventory days.

That reframing cuts both ways, and the honest version holds both halves:

  • The quality of the working capital is better than the ratio suggests. 84% of the ₹145.55cr of inventory is raw material and components, not unsold machines. It is funded by supplier credit and retained earnings, not debt. There is essentially no customer credit risk in it.
  • It is still real cash, and it scales. The CMD's own projection — "From INR100 crore business it rose to INR200 crore. From INR200 crore to INR300 crore. Now it will be INR450 crore. Next year, it will be INR600 crore. So, working capital, in terms of rupees will increase" (Q1FY27 call) — is an accurate description of a model that consumes cash as it grows.

One measurement trap worth knowing before you compare anything

There are two "cash flow from operations" figures for this company and they differ by a factor of two. Screener reports a CFO/operating-profit ratio of 48% for FY26; computed post-tax from the audited statement it is 26% (₹14.03cr ÷ ₹53.98cr). The difference is simply whether tax paid is deducted — Screener's ratio uses cash generated from operations of ₹26.09cr, before the ₹12.06cr tax outflow.

Neither is wrong. But they are not interchangeable, and a comparison that mixes one company's pre-tax ratio with another's post-tax ratio is meaningless. MY INFERENCE state which basis you are using, every time.

9. The price ladder, and where the volume actually is

Machine tools run from about ₹12 lakh to well over ₹3 crore, and the population at each price point is wildly uneven. Management sized the Indian market for turning machines on the Q1FY27 call, and the shape is worth seeing.

This is why the glamorous end of the market is not where a volume builder plays. Fewer than ten machines above ₹3 crore are made in India in a year. Management's analogy is exact: "Maruti will sell the most, Ferrari, or Mercedes, numbers will be less." The high end is also the end where imports win outright, and where — for five-axis machines specifically — export controls bite: FANUC and Mitsubishi will not supply five-axis controls into India, Siemens requires a long end-use agreement, and the CMD has said plainly that selling five-axis machines "will not be my business model… we will sell 1-2 machines a year." (Q1FY27 call)

10. Where margin comes from — and the three levers

Here is what ₹100 of FY26 revenue actually became. The stack reconciles exactly to the audited P&L.

Management names three margin levers, in this order (Q1FY27 call):

  1. Operating leverage. A large part of the cost base — the branch network, the technology centres, the 220-strong sales and service team — does not scale with units. More machines through the same overhead lifts margin. This is the lever that produced Q1FY27's +321bps.
  2. Backward integration. Moving components from bought to made, attacking the 61.5% material line. This is the lever that needs the new plant's machinery, and Section 4 explains why it works slowly.
  3. Mix — defence and higher-end machines. The one with the longest lead time and the least control: defence is only about ₹20cr of a ₹456cr order book, and management says tenders have been frozen — "since two years old tender is not yet open."

Why the margin does not simply rise with revenue, in management's own words

Growth brings its own costs before it brings leverage. The CMD's list: outsourced job work when internal component capacity is exhausted, inward and outward freight that scales with units, customs duty on a larger import bill, and pre-operating cost incurred a year ahead for the next year's capacity. "Almost 60% cost comes" from salary and job work together, outside material (Q1FY27 call).

That is why the FY26 margin was 16.2% against FY25's 16.0% despite 27% revenue growth — the branch network was being built at the same time. MY INFERENCE the spend came first and the operating leverage is arriving afterwards, which is what Q1FY27's margin jump on a flat realisation looks like.

11. The ceiling — what the shelf cannot fix

The shelf solves delivery speed. It does nothing about how many machines can be assembled, and the binding constraints are not on the assembly line.

Two things follow, and both are structural rather than fixable by working harder.

First, there is a revenue ceiling on the existing plant, and management has stated it. "If INR20 lakhs is coming, then visibility of INR500 crores is in this plant" (Q1FY27 call). At ~₹20 lakh a machine, 2,500 machines is ₹500cr and there is nothing beyond it without new floor space.

Second, the in-house component lines run well below assembly capacity, so growth mechanically increases outsourcing. Sheet metal at roughly 1,320 machines a year is about half of assembly capacity. Every machine beyond that has its sheet metal bought outside — and job work, together with salary, is one of the two biggest cost lines after material. MY INFERENCE This is the precise reason backward integration and capacity expansion are the same project rather than two: the new plant's stated purpose is de-bottlenecking and component machinery, not more assembly bays. Without it, higher volume actively dilutes the margin that volume is supposed to create.

The industry constraint that no plant solves

Roughly 20% of components — including the CNC control, the most valuable single item in the machine — are bought by every machine-tool builder in the world, from a small number of Japanese, German and Taiwanese suppliers. A builder's cost structure therefore has a floor set by companies it does not control, and its access to the high end is set by what those suppliers are willing to licence and export. For five-axis machines that access is currently restricted by end-use control regimes, not by engineering capability.

What is not disclosed

Stated as gaps, because they are gaps — not inferred around.

  • Unit volumes. Explicitly refused: "Number of machines we are not disclosing because some of the strategy and competitor are watching everything" (Q3FY26 call). Every machine count on this page is revenue ÷ average realisation.
  • Backward integration by value. Only the component count is given (40–45%, target 75–80%). The share of cost made in-house — the number that actually determines the margin lever — appears nowhere.
  • Order book by sector or segment. Declined on cost grounds. No cyclicality analysis of the book is possible from outside.
  • Revenue mix by price band. NEXA is given as "approximately 40%" of the order book and "39% to 40%" executed in Q1FY27, but the low/middle/high split is not quantified in any period.
  • Job work, freight and customs as separate cost lines. Named repeatedly on calls as the drivers of margin, aggregated in the statements.
  • Any quarterly balance sheet. Inventory, receivables and cash are visible only at half-year and full-year. This is the single biggest reporting gap for a business whose entire story is on the balance sheet.
  • The new plant's capacity. Asked directly, the CMD said the calculation was not finished and promised it "in Q3": "I will not give a figure on that assumption."
  • Export revenue and customer concentration. No geographic split appears in the FY26 Annual Report — consistent with immaterial exports — and no customer concentration is disclosed.
  • The value share of the CNC control. Universally understood in the industry to be the largest single component cost; never quantified by the company.

13. Glossary and sources

CNC
Computer Numerical Control — the computer that drives the cutting tool along a programmed path, replacing a machinist's handwheels.
Turning centre
A machine where the workpiece spins against a fixed tool. Makes round parts: shafts, bushes, pulleys.
Machining centre
A machine where the cutter spins against a clamped workpiece. Makes prismatic parts: housings, moulds, plates. "VMC" is the vertical variety.
Turn-mill centre
Does both in one clamping. Fewer setups, less accumulated error, higher price.
Double column
A large machining centre with the spindle carried on a bridge between two uprights, for big or heavy work. Macpower's "DCM" series.
Five-axis
A machine moving the tool along five axes at once, for complex contoured parts such as turbine blades. Restricted by export-control regimes.
Spindle
The rotating assembly that holds either the work (turning) or the cutter (milling). Macpower makes its own.
Turret
The indexing head holding several cutting tools, so a machine can change tools without a human.
Ball screw
A threaded shaft running in recirculating ball bearings, converting motor rotation into linear travel accurate to microns. Imported.
LM guideway
Linear-motion rails the moving slides run on. With the ball screw, these set the machine's accuracy. Imported.
Job work
Sending a manufacturing step to an outside workshop because internal capacity is full. A variable cost that rises with volume until in-house capacity catches up.
NEXA
Macpower's higher-specification product series — ~40% of the order book and of Q1FY27 revenue executed.
Book-to-bill
Orders received in a period ÷ revenue recognised in it. Above 1.0 means the order book is growing.
Cash conversion cycle
Inventory days + debtor days − payable days. How long a rupee is tied up between paying a supplier and being paid by a customer.
IMTMA / IMTEX
The Indian Machine Tool Manufacturers' Association, and the large trade exhibition it runs each January — a significant order-booking event for the industry.
GIDC
Gujarat Industrial Development Corporation — the state industrial estates. Macpower's plant is in the Metoda estate near Rajkot.
