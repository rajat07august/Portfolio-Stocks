How the conversion model actually works

An industry primer for Wheels India — why half this company's revenue includes the metal and half does not, and why that single fact makes its reported numbers misleading in three separate ways
Built 8-Sep-2026 · Sourced from Wheels India's official transcripts, investor decks and exchange filings on disk · Companion to Company Profiles/WHEELS.md

This page explains the industry, not the investment case. What follows is the mechanism: what a wheel maker does that is not making wheels, what it means when a customer hands you a two-tonne casting and asks you to machine it, why that arrangement makes revenue understate activity and asset turnover look terrible while margins improve — and why the equipment that does the work takes twelve to fifteen months to arrive before it earns anything at all.

1. What Wheels India actually makes

The name is half the business. Wheels India makes steel and aluminium wheels for trucks, buses, tractors and earthmovers — and alongside them machines large castings for wind turbines, fabricates structural parts, and builds hydraulic cylinders, air suspension systems and lift axles.

Those two halves are sold to broadly the same customers and run on completely different economics. The rest of this page is about the difference.

2. The conversion model — the fact everything else follows from

Here is the mechanism, and it is unusual enough to be worth stating slowly. In windmill machining, large-casting machining and parts of fabrication, the customer supplies the raw material. A wind-turbine maker sends Wheels India a large iron casting; Wheels India machines it to tolerance and sends it back, billing only for the machining. It never buys the casting, never carries it as inventory, and never books its value as revenue.

3. Three ways conversion distorts the reported numbers

Because the metal is missing from one side of the business, three of the most commonly used metrics for an auto-component company give the wrong answer for this one.

4. The flat-revenue puzzle, resolved

An analyst put the obvious question on the Q2FY26 call: revenue had been stuck near ₹1,050–1,150cr for fourteen quarters despite heavy capital spending. Where had the money gone?

"…your asset turnover ratio on conversion type of business tends to be more 1:1. At the same time, the EBITDA margins are clean double-digit… So, while the top line has not grown, the bottom line has grown faster than the top line, largely due to the fact that there's a lot of conversion business that's happened."
— Management, Q2FY26 call (Oct-2025)

5. What a wind turbine casting is, and why it needs machining

The conversion work that matters most is machining large castings for wind turbines — gearbox housings, hubs, main-shaft and bearing housings. These arrive as rough iron castings weighing tonnes, and they leave with bores, faces and bolt circles held to tolerances measured in hundredths of a millimetre.

6. Why this work is hard to enter

If the conversion economics are that attractive, the natural question is why every machine shop is not doing it. Three barriers, and none of them is money alone.

7. The capex-to-earnings lag

The consequence of a twelve-to-fifteen-month lead time is that spending and earning are separated by more than a year — and in between, the asset is on the balance sheet carrying depreciation while producing nothing.

8. The segment where it either shows up or does not

All of the above is theory until it appears in one number: Industrial Components EBIT. That segment houses the windmill machining, the fabrication, the construction wheels and the hydraulic cylinders — and for several quarters it was growing revenue while its profit went backwards, exactly as §7 predicts an asset in its depreciation-without-revenue phase would.

9. The other half — a pass-through that is not complete

Eighty-four percent of revenue is the wheel business, and it runs on the opposite model: Wheels India buys the steel, forms it, and sells the wheel. The metal price is supposed to pass through to the customer. Management's own position is that it does not, entirely.

10. Where the margin ambition actually comes from

Management has a stated ambition of a double-digit EBITDA margin against a blended 8.0% today. The unusual part is the route: it does not depend on growth.

12. What is not disclosed

  • The size of the conversion book in rupees — the biggest gap. Conversion economics are described qualitatively and repeatedly, but no figure is given for how much revenue is conversion versus material-based. Industrial Components at ~16% of revenue is an upper bound, not a measurement, because parts of that segment do carry material.
  • Margin by segment beyond EBIT. Segment EBIT is published, which is more than many peers give — but the conversion slice's margin is described only as "clean double-digit" and "healthy IT type," never quantified.
  • Which divisions are loss-making, and by how much. The double-digit margin ambition rests on them turning (§10), and they are not named or sized.
  • Capacity and utilisation in the machining business — so there is no way to judge how much of the ₹400–450cr FY27 capex is expansion versus replacement, or how full the existing assets are.
  • What changed to move FY27 capex guidance ~50% in one quarter. The number was revised; the reason was not given, and Q1 carries no Q&A.
  • Customer concentration. The end-market mix is disclosed; the customer mix is not, in a business whose conversion work depends on a small number of wind-turbine makers.

13. Glossary

Conversion business
Work where the customer supplies the raw material and the supplier bills only its processing. No metal in the supplier's revenue, no inventory, no metal-price risk.
Material-based business
The opposite: the supplier buys the metal, and its price flows through both cost and revenue. The wheel business.
Pass-through
Recovering a change in input cost from the customer. Management states its recovery is not complete over the longer term.
Asset turnover
Revenue per rupee of assets. Structurally ~1:1 in conversion work and 3–4x in material-based work — a definitional difference, not a performance one.
Large-casting machining
Machining multi-tonne iron castings to fine tolerance. The core of Wheels India's conversion work.
WEG
Wind Energy Generator. Offshore WEG castings are larger again and correspondingly harder to machine.
Gearbox housing / hub
Large wind-turbine castings that hold shafts and bearings in alignment. Bores and faces must be machined precisely.
Industrial Components
Wheels India's reporting segment containing windmill machining, fabrication, construction wheels and hydraulic cylinders — where the conversion economics live.
Automotive Components
The other segment: steel and aluminium wheels, air suspension and lift axles. 84% of Q1FY27 revenue.
Air suspension
An air-spring system replacing steel leaf springs, sold into buses. Higher value per vehicle from an existing customer.
Lift axle
An axle that can be raised when a truck runs unladen, cutting tyre wear and fuel use.
Segment EBIT
Operating profit of one reporting segment. For this company, the number where conversion work becomes visible.
