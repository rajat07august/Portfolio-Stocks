1. What a drug company is actually buying

A biotech in Boston or Basel invents a molecule. It has twenty employees, venture funding and no factory — and it has no intention of building one. Big pharma, meanwhile, has spent two decades selling its plants. Somebody still has to actually make the drug. That somebody is a CRDMO: a Contract Research, Development and Manufacturing Organisation.

The three letters matter separately. Research is working out how to make a molecule nobody has made before. Development is proving that route works repeatably at increasing scale. Manufacturing is supplying it commercially, for years, once the drug is approved. Many contract manufacturers do only the last one. Anthem does all three, and the "R" is what gets it in early.

3. The funnel — and why most of it dies

A CRDMO's pipeline is not an order book. It is a population of programmes at different stages, most of which will never reach a market. The business is built on the survivors.

4. Chemistry and biology — the two lanes most peers pick between

There are two fundamentally different ways to make a drug substance. You can synthesise it — build the molecule step by step in a reactor from simpler chemicals. Or you can grow it — put an engineered organism in a fermenter and let biology assemble it. These need different plants, different scientists and different regulatory experience.

Most Indian CRDMOs pick one. Anthem runs both — 425 kilolitres of custom synthesis and 142 KL of fermentation — which is what lets it accept the molecule classes the industry is moving toward.

5. Why the customer cannot leave

This is the mechanism that makes a commercial CRDMO contract an annuity rather than a purchase order — and it is regulatory, not commercial.

6. Why the quarter is not a control variable

A CRDMO ships when the customer says ship. Batches are large, campaigns are scheduled months ahead, and a delivery that slips from the last week of March into the first week of April moves a material sum between two quarters without anything changing in the business.

7. Where a 40% margin comes from

Contract manufacturing is not supposed to earn 40% EBITDA margins. Most of the sector lives in the twenties. Anthem's answer has a name and a number attached to it: material margin — what is left of revenue after the physical inputs — and it has risen three years running.

8. The second business — and why it exists

Alongside the CRDMO sits Specialty Ingredients: probiotics, enzymes and nutraceutical actives, sold largely B2B into India. It is lower-margin and much less glamorous — and it is there for a structural reason that §6 explains.

9. Who the customers are, and where they are

Two facts sit together and pull in opposite directions: the customer base is highly concentrated, and it is weighted to Europe rather than America.

10. The decay clock the CEO named himself

The most useful sentence about this industry's risk was said by Anthem's own Chairman and MD, unprompted, about his own moat.

"What you might consider specialty today will become fine chemicals much faster. A fine chemical will go towards commoditization much faster than before."

12. What is not disclosed

  • The order book in rupees — the biggest gap. Only a percentage is given ("60% of the order book for the full year"), with no base, no ageing and no comparative. It cannot be tracked from one quarter to the next, which matters enormously in a business whose quarterly revenue is set by delivery timing.
  • Customer names. The three customers that are 56.71% of revenue are identified only as A, B and C — so their end-market, therapeutic area and patent runway are all unknown.
  • Revenue by molecule or by stage. The funnel is given as counts, not as rupees, so there is no way to see how much revenue rests on the 14 commercial molecules versus development work.
  • Capacity utilisation. 425 KL and 142 KL are installed figures; how full they are is not published.
  • The split between the "R", the "D" and the "M". The three activities have very different economics and are reported as one CRDMO line.
  • Pricing structure — whether development work is billed at cost-plus, FTE rates or milestones is not described, so the margin improvement in §7 cannot be attributed between pricing, yield and integration.

13. Glossary

CRDMO
Contract Research, Development and Manufacturing Organisation — a company that takes someone else's molecule from discovery through to commercial supply.
CDMO
The same without the research: development and manufacturing only. The missing "R" is the distinction Anthem trades on.
API
Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient — the substance in a drug that actually does something. What a CRDMO makes.
NCE / NBE
New Chemical Entity (a synthesised small molecule) and New Biological Entity (produced biologically). Anthem is among the few Indian firms taking both.
Custom synthesis
Building a molecule step by step in reactors. Measured here in kilolitres of reactor volume.
Fermentation
Growing a molecule using an engineered organism in a fermenter. A different plant and a different science from synthesis.
Biocatalysis
Using enzymes as tools inside a chemical route — where the chemistry and biology lanes meet.
Flow chemistry
Running a reaction continuously rather than in batches. Better control, lower cost, smaller footprint.
ADC
Antibody-Drug Conjugate — a targeting antibody chemically linked to a potent drug. Needs both chemistry and biology.
Oligonucleotide / RNAi
Short nucleic-acid drugs that act on genetic material rather than on proteins. A fast-growing and demanding class.
Peptide
A short chain of amino acids — the class behind much recent metabolic-drug growth.
Material margin
Revenue less the cost of physical inputs. The cleanest measure of whether backward integration and yield work are paying off.
Phase III
The last and largest stage of clinical trials before approval is sought. A molecule here is close to becoming an annuity — or to failing expensively.
Commercial molecule
An approved drug in ongoing production. Anthem has 14, each named in a customer's regulatory filing.
