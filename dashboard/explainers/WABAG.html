Selling a lifecycle, not a plant

An industry primer for VA Tech Wabag: what a water-treatment plant physically is, why the company that builds it is usually not the company that sells the water, how a bid is won on the cost of electricity twenty years from now — and why three of the numbers in its results (the margin percentage, the EBITDA, and the order book) each mislead in a different direction
Built 23-Sep-2026 · Sourced from WABAG's FY26 Annual Report (consolidated statements and notes read by coordinate extraction), nine earnings-call transcripts (Nov-2024 → Aug-2026), seven investor presentations, and NSE filings · Companion to Company Profiles/WABAG.md

This page explains the industry, not the investment case. It carries no valuation, no conviction and no target; those live in the profile. What follows is the mechanism: what the product physically is, who pays for it and on what terms, which part of the work actually earns money, and how to read the three numbers in WABAG's recent results that are easiest to misread.

1. The product, physically: a train of stages, not a building

Almost everyone pictures a water project as concrete: tanks, pipes, a pumping station. That picture is the part WABAG increasingly does not do. What it sells is the treatment scheme — the decision about which physical and biological stages the water passes through, in what order, at what pressure, and at what energy cost per cubic metre.

A plant is a train: a sequence of stages, each removing something the next stage cannot handle. Get the order or the sizing wrong and the expensive stage at the end clogs, which is the whole engineering problem in one sentence.

The two trains above are the company's two main product families, and they are genuinely different businesses wearing the same word.

  • Desalination separates salt from seawater by forcing it through a membrane at high pressure. The dominant running cost is electricity, which is why an energy-recovery device — a pump that reclaims pressure from the reject brine — is a core part of the train rather than an accessory.
  • Sewage treatment uses bacteria to eat dissolved organic waste, then separates the bacteria out again. The dominant engineering problem is biological stability, not pressure. Its by-product, sludge, is turned into biogas: at the Pagla plant in Dhaka, digestion is designed to supply "up to 40% of its energy demand." FY26 AR, project review.

MY INFERENCE The reason to hold both in one company is that they are sold to the same buyer under the same contract structures, not because the technology overlaps. A desalination engineer and a sewage-process engineer do not do the same job.

The number that makes the market exist

Management's framing is that treated water is cheap relative to what people already pay. The CMD's figures: desalinated water at "8 paise a litre" and recycled water at "maybe 5 paise to 6 paise a litre", against ₹20 for a litre of bottled water. Annual Investor Meet, 25-May-2026.

MY INFERENCE These are management's cost assertions and are not reconciled to any tariff in the filings. The useful part is not the precision but the structure they describe: seawater and sewage are the only two water sources that do not fail in a drought, because neither depends on rainfall. WABAG calls this "manufactured water." That is the demand case for the whole industry, and it is why coastal cities and the Gulf build desalination capacity rather than more reservoirs.

2. Who does what between the sea and the tap

The single most useful thing to understand about WABAG is how narrow its slice of the chain is, and how deliberately it was narrowed. The CFO's own description: "We are a technology system integrator, not a EPC organization." Q1FY27 call, 13-Aug-2026.

Read the two side arrows, because they are where the money goes. WABAG buys the equipment and it hands the concrete to a partner. It designs the scheme, specifies and procures the kit, supervises erection and commissioning, proves the performance guarantee, and then — on a growing share of contracts — operates the plant for between one and twenty years.

Civil construction passes through its accounts at cost. This is stated plainly and repeatedly:

"Construction for us is a pass-through. Our idea would remain that to see if we can have more design build orders. But of course, even when we take design build, operate orders, since construction is a pass-through, effectively, our margins are made only from design and build."
CFO, Q1FY27 call, 13-Aug-2026.

That sentence is the key to §7, and it is worth holding on to: a large part of WABAG's reported revenue is work on which it does not intend to earn a margin at all.

The risk profile is managed by subcontracting regardless of what the contract is called; only the accounting differs, which is precisely why the margin percentage moves around.

3. The mechanism: what is sold is a lifecycle cost

This is the section that, once understood, makes the rest of the business obvious.

In the Gulf — now roughly half the order book — the water utility does not buy a plant. It buys water, under a twenty-to-twenty-five-year concession, from a developer who finds the capital, builds the plant, runs it, and sells the output at an agreed price per cubic metre. WABAG is usually not the developer. It is the technology and construction contract inside the developer's bid.

The consequence is unusual and worth stating precisely: WABAG's commercial problem is not to win a tender, but to make somebody else win theirs. Rohan Mittal, who runs the Middle East and Africa region, put the sequence in one sentence:

"So basically it's a life cycle cost. It's not your capex, it's not your opex, it is a life cycle cost which makes the developer competitive when they take concession contracts which are 20 years and 25 years. Only when they are competitive, we will win the jobs. First they have to win the job, so we ensure that they win the job so that after they win, we have an exclusivity with them, we win the job."
Annual Investor Meet, 25-May-2026.

Because the scoring is lifecycle cost, the competitive lever is the electricity bill twenty years out, not the price of the steel. This is why an engineering choice that looks trivial is treated as commercially decisive:

"We have to find that thin line, you know, how much whether we should go for IE3, whether we should go for IE4, whether we should go IE5 motors. We go to that details on motor efficiencies, 0.5% to 1%, and we add that value because one big advantage that we have… we have 500 engineers sitting and engineering plants at house."
Annual Investor Meet, 25-May-2026. IE3/IE4/IE5 are international motor-efficiency classes; see glossary.

And it is why management is explicit that it will accept a worse capital cost to win on the total: "Maybe the capex might be little higher than others, but on a whole, our power, our opex, and capex has to be minimum."

Why owning the operating business feeds the bidding business

WABAG claims to be the world's third-largest private water operator, and management's argument is that running plants is what makes its bids credible: "there are only people who build plants or only people who operate plants… there are not many companies or EPC contractors who have operated the plants who understand what will help my developer to be competitive in opex."

The stated result in one market: "In the last six STPs, we've won three." Annual Investor Meet, 25-May-2026. Win-rate and ranking claims are management's and are not independently verified.

MY INFERENCE This is a real feedback loop and it is the strongest structural argument in the business: operating data is an input to pricing, and only operators have it. It is also slow to build and cannot be bought, which is what distinguishes it from the technology claim — patents expire, and management says so.

One more structural feature of the technology: WABAG does not license any of it. "We use our technologies for our captive use, we don't sell our technologies, we don't license out our technologies." The stated holding is "more than 125 patents and trademarks", with R&D in Vienna and Chennai. The practical effect management gives is a cost one — a component competitors must buy in, WABAG makes — rather than an exclusivity one.

4. The filter that comes before the engineering

Before WABAG evaluates whether it can build something, it asks who is paying. This is the most consequential policy in the company, and it is stated as an absolute:

"As you know, payment security is a biggest factor when we select our projects… We don't take projects which are funded by state government or municipalities because we consider that as a big risk."
CMD, Annual Investor Meet, 25-May-2026.

Two things follow from this that are easy to miss.

First, it disqualifies most of the Indian market by construction. Water is a state subject in India. A rule that excludes state and municipal funding excludes the majority of domestic tenders, leaving only multilateral-funded work (World Bank, ADB, JICA, KfW, AIIB) and central schemes such as Namami Gange and AMRUT. That is a deliberate trade of volume for collection.

Second, the discipline extends to what counts as an order at all. Contracts signed but not yet backed by an advance payment or a letter of credit are held outside the order book as "framework contracts" — ₹611 cr of them at 31-Mar-2026, disclosed as such in the annual report. FY26 AR, Note 23B. In Q1FY27 the Libya framework contract was removed entirely, which the CFO described as done "prudently." Both the carve-out and the removal make the headline backlog smaller than it could legitimately have been.

The chain this filter produced

Filter for payment security → get paid → go net cash → get rated AA- → obtain bank guarantees more cheaply and in larger size → qualify to bid for larger international jobs. MY FRAMING

The guarantee step is not incidental. An EPC contractor must post performance and advance-payment guarantees to be allowed to bid at all, so the credit rating is a bidding input, not just a financing cost. WABAG's bank charges — mostly guarantee commission, not interest — ran ₹499 Mn in FY26 against interest on borrowings of only ₹230 Mn. FY26 AR, Note 28. Reading bank charges as interest expense, and therefore as leverage, would be a mistake; this point was raised and correctly resolved on the ValuePickr thread.

5. The ₹100 cost stack: a buyer of equipment, not a maker of it

Every line below is a disclosed line in the FY26 consolidated accounts, and they sum exactly to revenue.

Three readings matter.

The largest single cost is equipment WABAG does not make. ₹44.48 of every ₹100 is bought-in material: membranes, high-pressure pumps, motors, pressure vessels, energy-recovery devices. Add civil at ₹14.61 and erection at ₹2.37 and roughly ₹61 of every ₹100 is bought from somebody else. This is what "asset-light" actually means here — not that the business is small, but that the capital equipment sits in the customer's plant and the manufacturing sits in the supply chain.

The engineering that is supposedly the product costs ₹0.15. The "engineering costs" line is ₹58 Mn on ₹39,442 Mn of revenue. MY INFERENCE This is not evidence that engineering is unimportant — it is evidence that the engineers are employees, so their cost sits in the ₹3,004 Mn employee line (₹7.62 per ₹100), and the project-level "engineering costs" line captures only bought-in design work. The ~2,000 engineers management describes are a fixed cost carried between projects, not a project input.

Provisioning moves the answer by more than a point. Inside "other expenses" sits bad and doubtful debts of ₹884 Mn in FY26 against a ₹197 Mn release in FY25 — a swing of over ₹1,080 Mn, or 2.7 points of margin, between two consecutive years. FY26 AR, Note 30. Any comparison of FY25 and FY26 margins that does not account for this is comparing two different things.

6. How an order becomes revenue — and why that takes years

Revenue is recognised on percentage of completion: as costs are incurred against a contract, a proportionate share of the contract price is booked as revenue, whether or not anything has been invoiced. This is the reason the balance sheet in §10 looks the way it does, and it is flagged as a Key Audit Matter by the auditors.

The annual report states the two clocks directly, and this single sentence does more work than any chart:

"Construction contracts are progressively executed over a period of upto 3.5 years and based on specific project schedules. Operation and maintenance contracts are expected to be executed over a period of 1 to 20 years, primarily invoiced on a monthly basis."
FY26 AR, Note 23B — transaction price allocated to remaining performance obligations.

The elapsed time from a bid to the first rupee of revenue is long and has several gates, each of which has visibly slipped on real projects:

Why the order book grows even when nothing is won

The annual report reconciles the backlog for the year, and one line in it is easy to miss:

₹577 cr of FY26's backlog growth came from currency movement and scope variation, not from winning work. With about half the book denominated in foreign currency, a falling rupee mechanically inflates the order book in rupee terms. MY INFERENCE The same force is at work in §8, and it means the backlog and the forex gain are not independent facts — they have a common cause.

7. 🔴 Reading the numbers (I): the margin percentage is partly a mix artefact

Nearly every business has one metric that misleads. WABAG has three, and this is the first — the one that is structural rather than circumstantial.

Because construction is passed through at cost, an EPC-heavy quarter adds revenue and cost in equal measure and no margin at all. The margin percentage therefore falls when the mix shifts toward construction, even if every project is performing exactly as bid.

The lower panel is not a model. It is the reported consolidated numbers for two quarters:

  • Revenue rose from ₹7,340 Mn to ₹8,868 Mn — up ₹1,528 Mn, or 20.8%.
  • Cost of sales rose from ₹5,230 Mn to ₹6,758 Mn — up ₹1,528 Mn.
  • Gross profit was ₹2,110 Mn in both quarters, identical to the million.
  • Gross margin therefore fell from 28.75% to 23.79% — down 495 basis points — without a single rupee of gross profit being lost.

Management's stated explanation is project mix, and it is specific about which projects: "a majority of the revenues would be EPC. Our top 5 projects would be largely EPC." The named execution-phase jobs are Perur, Pagla and Al Haer — all construction-heavy, all mid-build. Q1FY27 call, 13-Aug-2026.

What this does and does not establish

MY INFERENCE The identical gross profit is consistent with the entire revenue increase being zero-margin pass-through work. It does not prove it, and I want to be exact about the limit: WABAG does not publish an EP-versus-EPC revenue split, so the increment cannot be decomposed. The CFO said so directly when asked for the quarter's split — "as a project company, it's very difficult to measure every quarter what would be the EP, EPC… even if I give you metrics, it may not be the right representation."

What it does establish is the direction of the error: reading a 495bp gross-margin fall as deteriorating project economics assumes the denominator is comparable between the two quarters, and on management's own description of the contract structure, it is not.

The same logic runs in reverse, and is the part most likely to be forgotten: a shift toward EP work will raise the margin percentage while adding less revenue. Growth and margin are partly substitutes in this business, by design.

8. 🔴 Reading the numbers (II): the forex line sits inside EBITDA

WABAG bills roughly half its revenue in foreign currency. Management includes exchange gains and losses inside the EBITDA it reports and guides to. The audited P&L, by contrast, shows "Foreign Exchange Gains" as its own line, above other income — so the two presentations are genuinely different, and the annual report gives the number needed to move between them. FY26 AR, Consolidated Statement of Profit and Loss.

The gap between the blue and red bars is the whole of the disagreement, and it has widened every quarter but one.

Management's case, stated fairly

"We don't understand the vocab of margins, excluding forex, if we have more than 52% of the revenues coming from international geographies, the forex gains are going to be there… we don't have forex loans. … These are all absolutely transactional. You raise an invoice, you collect the money, you get forex gains. You book a cost, you pay the money, you get forex gains or loss. So these are part and parcel of the business."
CFO, Q1FY27 call, 13-Aug-2026, replying to Mihir Vyas.

This argument has real merit and should not be dismissed. The gains arise from invoices and payments in the ordinary course, not from treasury positions or borrowing in foreign currency. A company earning half its revenue abroad will always have a transaction-exchange line, and excluding it entirely would understate a genuine part of operations.

The CFO also makes a fair consistency point: "I don't remember that in the last year first quarter somebody adjusted for the forex loss when there was one." Q1FY26 did carry a ₹136 Mn forex loss, and stripping it out raises that quarter's margin to 14.9%. The chart above shows both years on both bases for exactly this reason.

MY INFERENCE Three observations that follow, none of which requires taking a side:

  • The line is large relative to profit. Q1FY27's ₹368 Mn gain compares with ₹901 Mn of PAT. Taxed at the group's ~25% rate, it is roughly 31% of the quarter's profit.
  • It is a directional bet on a currency, not a spread. A transaction gain arises when the rupee falls between invoicing and collection. It is real cash — and it is also not repeatable at a stable exchange rate, which is a different property from the rest of EBITDA. A flat rupee removes it; a rising rupee reverses it, as Q1FY26 shows.
  • The guidance is given on the basis that includes it. FY27 EBITDA guidance is "13% to 14%" reported. On the ex-forex basis the last four quarters have printed 10.7%, 12.6%, 11.1% and 9.0%. Both statements are true at once, which is the entire reason this section exists.

Ex-forex EBITDA = management's EBITDA less the forex gain, or plus the loss, shown on the same deck line. The percentages use the same revenue denominator in both cases, so only the numerator changes. Every figure in the chart is computed from the quarterly decks; none is read off a chart image.

9. 🔴 Reading the numbers (III): the order book is not years of revenue

The backlog at 30-Jun-2026 was ₹19,394 cr, about 4.9 times FY26 revenue, and it is the number most often quoted about this company. The trap is arithmetic: 4.9 times revenue is not 4.9 years of revenue, because the book contains two populations with completely different conversion speeds.

Backlog from the Q1FY27 deck (EPC ₹127,280 Mn, O&M ₹66,662 Mn). Revenue split from the same deck's segment table (EPC ₹7,256 Mn, O&M ₹1,593 Mn). Contract lengths from FY26 AR Note 23B. Annualising one quarter is crude — Q4 is seasonally the largest — and is used here only to compare the two halves with each other.

The two halves behave in opposite ways, and each is misread in its own direction.

The O&M book cannot be accelerated. A twenty-year contract invoiced monthly converts at one-twentieth a year no matter how well the company executes. This is why O&M revenue grew only 7% year on year while the O&M backlog grew 26% — and the CFO's explanation is structural rather than apologetic: "O&M by structure will grow at this pace because projects have to be completed for it to move into the O&M phase." Ghaziabad and Digha-Kankarbagh moved into O&M during the year; Perur follows next year.

The EPC book, by contrast, implies growth. At 4.4 times the current EPC run-rate but with contracts that run at most 3.5 years, the EPC backlog cannot be delivered at today's execution rate — MY INFERENCE the arithmetic requires EPC revenue to rise, or the book to age. That is a genuine constraint on the business and it points the opposite way from the O&M reading.

The honest summary of the order book

A third of the backlog is an annuity: long, predictable, low-risk, and slow. Two-thirds is project work that must be executed within three and a half years and that carries the construction pass-through of §7. They are different assets with different conversion clocks, and the single 4.9× multiple averages them into a number that describes neither.

To be fair to the company: WABAG has never claimed the book equals years of revenue. It discloses the split, the contract durations and the segment revenue, all of which are used above. The misreading is the reader's to avoid.

10. The counterweight: the balance sheet is the business

"Asset-light" is said often enough about WABAG that it is worth looking at what the balance sheet actually contains. The CMD's version: "we don't invest in real assets, real estate, buildings, offices. We don't invest in equipment; we don't invest in manufacturing. We are a design technology company, which is all brain here." That is accurate. It is also only half the sentence.

The CFO completes it, and this is the more useful formulation:

"…as you know we are an asset-light organization, our only asset is working capital. And if we do not even invest three months of working capital into the business, how do we even grow?"
CFO, Q1FY27 call, 13-Aug-2026.

The plant and equipment is genuinely negligible — ₹632 Mn, against depreciation of just ₹62 Mn a year and capital commitments of nil at both year-ends. FY26 AR, Note 46(b). But receivables, retentions and unbilled work together come to ₹43,553 Mn — 110% of a full year's revenue. The asset base was not eliminated; it changed form, from plant into money owed.

12. What is not disclosed

  • 🔴 The EP versus EPC revenue split. The single most useful number for reading §7, and it is not published in any period. Asked directly for the quarter's split, the CFO declined on the grounds that it would not be representative. Without it, the pass-through effect on margin can be described but not sized.
  • 🔴 Margin by contract type. The statement that design-build is "better in margin" than EPC is never quantified, in any filing or call.
  • Volumes. Nothing is published in cubic metres or MLD delivered, so there is no revenue-per-unit series and no way to separate price from volume in any segment.
  • The O&M margin, and whether it differs from project margin. O&M is 18% of revenue and 34% of the backlog, and its profitability is not separately reported.
  • Project-level profitability of the mega jobs. Yanbu was won by 0.27% and Doha is of comparable size; neither has a disclosed margin, and segment reporting is by geography rather than by project.
  • The split of forex between the two effects. Transaction gains on receivables and translation of foreign subsidiaries are different things; only the combined P&L line is given. Translation differences of ₹688 Mn sit separately in other comprehensive income, but no reconciliation between them is published.
  • How much of the ECL movement is a write-off rather than a P&L reversal. The FY25 utilisation of ₹864 Mn is not decomposed in the AR, so the extent to which FY25's margin was helped by releases cannot be computed exactly.

13. Glossary · Sources

Glossary

MLD
Million litres per day — the capacity unit for a water or sewage plant. Perur is 400 MLD; a 45 MLD plant serves a large district.
Desalination / SWRO
Making fresh water from seawater. SWRO is sea-water reverse osmosis: pushing seawater through a membrane at high pressure so that water passes and salt does not.
Membrane
The filter that does the separating. Polymer membranes are standard and are replaced every three to four years; ceramic ones cost more but can be cleaned by firing in a furnace.
Energy recovery device (ERD)
A pump that captures pressure from the reject brine and returns it to the incoming feed. It exists because electricity is the dominant running cost of desalination.
Brine
The concentrated salt stream left over after desalination, returned to the sea.
STP / WTP / ETP
Sewage, water and effluent treatment plant respectively — municipal sewage, drinking water, and industrial waste streams.
TTRO
Tertiary treatment reverse osmosis: polishing already-treated sewage to a standard fit for industrial reuse.
ZLD
Zero liquid discharge — treating an industrial effluent so completely that nothing is released, usually by evaporating the last of it.
UPW
Ultrapure water, with essentially all dissolved salts removed. Required by semiconductor and solar-cell fabrication.
Activated sludge / SBR / MBR
Biological treatment methods in which bacteria consume dissolved organic waste, distinguished by how the bacteria are held and separated afterwards.
Lamella clarifier
A settling tank with stacked inclined plates, used where there is not enough land for a conventional one.
EP · EPC · DB · DBO
Contract scopes — Engineering and Procurement; the same plus Construction; Design-Build; Design-Build-Operate. The presence or absence of the C is what §7 turns on.
RFOMT
Refurbish, finance, operate, maintain and transfer — a concession over an existing plant, as at Kodungaiyur for twenty years.
HAM
Hybrid annuity model: the contractor funds part of the capital and is repaid in instalments over the concession.
Developer
The party that wins a concession, raises the capital and sells the output. In the Gulf, WABAG's customer is often the developer rather than the utility.
Lifecycle cost
Capital cost plus the whole operating cost of a concession period. The basis on which developer-led bids are scored, and therefore the thing WABAG is really competing on.
IE3 / IE4 / IE5
International efficiency classes for electric motors; a higher number means less electricity for the same work, at a higher purchase price. Choosing between them is a lifecycle-cost decision.
L1 / lowest evaluated bidder
The bidder ranked first after technical and commercial evaluation. Publicly announced in Gulf tenders, but not yet an award — the gap has run as long as ten months.
LoA
Letter of Award — the client's formal award, which still precedes contract signature and effectiveness.
PGTR
Performance Guarantee Test Run: the period in which a finished plant must demonstrate its contracted output and quality before handover.
Percentage of completion
Recognising revenue as costs are incurred against a contract, rather than when invoices are raised. The reason unbilled dues exist.
Unbilled dues
Revenue recognised under percentage of completion that has not yet been invoiced to the customer.
Retention
A share of each invoice the customer withholds until the plant has performed for a contracted period. It appears as a non-current receivable.
Framework contract
A signed contract not yet backed by an advance payment or letter of credit. WABAG holds these outside the reported order book.
Performance / advance-payment guarantee
A bank's undertaking to pay the client if the contractor fails. Required to bid; its cost appears as bank charges, not interest.
ECL
Expected credit loss — the provision carried against receivables that may not be collected.
Multilateral funding
Money from the World Bank, ADB, JICA, KfW or AIIB. The payment-security test in §4.
