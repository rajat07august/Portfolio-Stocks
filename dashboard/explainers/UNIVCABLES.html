How the power-cable business actually works

An industry primer for Universal Cables: what a high-voltage cable physically is, why each step up the voltage ladder is a different business, why the turnkey jobs tie up cash, and why the headline profit is partly someone else's
Built 13-Oct-2026 · The company holds no earnings calls, so this page is sourced from its FY22–FY26 annual reports, results filings and press releases (Feb-2024 → Aug-2026), Reg 30 capacity filings and CARE rating rationales · Companion to Company Profiles/UNIVCABLES.md

This page explains the industry and the mechanism, not the investment case. An underground power cable looks like a thick black hose. Inside, it is a precisely layered object whose hardest layer, the insulation, gets thicker and less forgiving with every step up in voltage. That one fact explains most of this business: why only a few plants can make 220–400 kV cable, why Universal built vertical curing towers, why a "kilometre of capacity" is not a fixed unit, and why the top of the market is sold as a turnkey project that ties up cash for up to two years.

1. The product, physically: a cable is a stack of layers

INDUSTRY BACKGROUND A modern power cable is built outward from the centre, and each layer has one job. The conductor (copper or aluminium strands) carries the current. A thin semi-conducting screen smooths the electric field at the conductor's surface. The insulation, almost always cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE), holds back the voltage. A second semi-conducting screen sits outside it, then a metallic screen or sheath that carries fault current and, at high voltage, keeps water out. An outer sheath protects everything from the soil.

Universal makes this product across the whole range, from 1.1 kV to 400 kV, plus PVC- and rubber-insulated cables up to 11 kV (CARE, Feb-2026; AR FY26 Directors' Report). The Goa plant makes the small end: winding wires, building wires and flexible cables.

2. The voltage ladder: four businesses in one plant

The single most useful way to understand a cable maker is to ask which rungs of the voltage ladder it can climb. Each rung has different buyers, different competition and a different qualification barrier.

The key point: the bottom two rungs are crowded and the top rung is not. MY INFERENCE Almost anyone with an extruder can make LV cable, and branded players compete on distribution. At EHV the barrier is a system prequalification test that runs for about a year INDUSTRY BACKGROUND (IEC 62067), a licensed design and a plant that can cure thick insulation without distorting it. Universal's directors claim "an industry leading market share in the Extra High Voltage (EHV) Power Cable business in India" (AR FY26). The share is not quantified. EHV revenue grew about 33% in FY26 (AR FY26), faster than any other cable line.

3. Why EHV cable is made in a vertical tower

INDUSTRY BACKGROUND After the three inner layers are extruded together, the polyethylene has to be cross-linked (cured) under heat and pressure so that it does not soften when the cable runs hot. MV cable is usually cured in a long tube that hangs in a shallow curve, a catenary line. For thin insulation that works well. For the very thick wall an EHV cable needs, gravity is a problem: while the hot plastic is still soft it can sag towards the bottom of a horizontal-ish run, leaving the insulation thinner on top than underneath. At 400 kV, an off-centre wall is a weak spot.

Two consequences follow. First, EHV output is gated by tower time, not by how many extruders a plant owns: INDUSTRY BACKGROUND thick insulation has to pass slowly through the tower, and large EHV cable is then degassed for days before sheathing. Second, the FY24 upgrade shows up in the accounts: FY24 revenue fell to ₹2,020 Cr from ₹2,202 Cr, and the company attributed the drop "primarily" to the planned VCV shutdown (AR FY24).

4. From copper rod to a commissioned link

The last three boxes are why a cable company becomes a construction company at the top of the market. INDUSTRY BACKGROUND An EHV link is only as good as its joints, and a utility buying a 400 kV underground circuit wants one party responsible for the whole system, not a drum supplier and a separate contractor blaming each other. FACT Universal commissioned what it describes as the first made-in-India 400 kV underground HVAC cable project, 11.34 route-km in Tamil Nadu, in FY25, with a second 9.50 route-km project in progress (FY25 press release, 22-May-2025).

5. Two ways to sell: a drum, or a finished link

Universal reports one segment, but its own revenue note splits sales into two kinds that behave very differently (AR FY26, note 42):

This is the mechanism behind the company's cash flow. MY INFERENCE Every rupee of EPC growth needs working capital for most of two years, and the company has chosen to fund it with debt. So the faster the turnkey book grows, the more borrowing rises, however profitable each job is. In FY26 operating cash flow was ₹63 Cr against ₹260.6 Cr of operating profit, and borrowings rose from ₹849 Cr to ₹1,177 Cr (Screener).

6. Capacity in kilometres, and the capex that buys it

Why the kilometre misleads. MY INFERENCE A kilometre of 400 kV cable carries a thick insulation wall, a metallic water barrier and, often, a turnkey installation. A kilometre of LV cable is a fraction of that weight and value. A plant's kilometre capacity therefore depends entirely on what it is assumed to make, which is why the filings quote a range "based on product mix". The May-2026 EHV modernisation (₹73 Cr) was explicitly expected "not [to] alter the production capacity materially" (board outcome, 23-May-2026): it buys precision and export certifications, not kilometres.

7. Where ₹100 of revenue goes

Three things stand out. Three-quarters of revenue is metal and polymer, so a move in copper or aluminium reaches revenue quickly and margin only if the price was fixed before the move. Interest takes almost as much as everything left over after depreciation, which is the cost of funding the turnkey book and the capex (§5). And the company's own "EBITDA" includes other income: its FY26 figure of ₹289.0 Cr is the ₹260.6 Cr operating profit plus ₹28.3 Cr of other income. Its "~10%" margin guidance for FY27 is on that inclusive basis MY RECONCILIATION.

8. Reading the numbers: the profit that isn't from cables

This is the section that changes how every headline figure should be read. Universal has no subsidiaries, but it holds two equity-accounted investments: 29.15% of Vindhya Telelinks (a listed telecom- and solar-cable and EPC company) and 26.14% of Birla Furukawa Fibre Optics (BFFO, an unlisted optical-fibre maker run with Furukawa Electric). Under equity accounting, Universal's share of their profits is added to its own in the consolidated accounts, and its share of their net worth sits on its consolidated balance sheet. None of their revenue or cash appears.

What this means for the ratios you see

  • P/E: the market cap is divided by consolidated profit, ~40% of which is associate profit (FY26). In Q1FY27 the associate/JV share was ₹38.9 Cr of ₹70.1 Cr consolidated PAT, over half (results filing, 7-Aug-2026).
  • ROE and ROCE: 54% of consolidated net worth is the associates' book, which earns no operating return in Universal's P&L. That is why the consolidated ROE (8.9%) is lower than the cable business's own (~11% in FY26) MY ARITHMETIC.
  • Cash: associate profit is not cash. Universal received ₹5.5 Cr of dividends from Vindhya in FY26 (AR FY26 related-party note) against ₹46.6 Cr of profit booked.
  • Restatements travel: in Aug-2026 Vindhya restated five years of accounts after it was finally able to consolidate three NBFC subsidiaries, and Universal's FY26 consolidated PAT moved from ₹163.1 Cr to ₹164.7 Cr with it (results filing, note 3).

9. The ownership loop

The associate is not only something Universal owns. It also owns Universal.

MY INFERENCE A loop like this has two practical effects for a reader of the accounts. First, part of the consolidated book is circular: Vindhya's net worth includes its stake in Universal, and Universal books 29.15% of that net worth. Second, any market valuation that adds "the value of the Vindhya stake" to the cable business partly counts Universal twice, because Vindhya's share price reflects its 23.85% of Universal. The company's own consolidation note flags a "cross holding between VTL & UCL" adjustment of ₹43.1 Cr (AR FY26, note 52).

11. Capacitors, inputs and the honest counterweight

The capacitor business

INDUSTRY BACKGROUND Motors, transformers and long cables draw "reactive" power that does no useful work but still loads the grid. Capacitor banks supply it locally, improving the power factor and stabilising voltage; harmonic filters and static var generators clean up the distortion modern electronic loads create. FACT Universal makes capacitors, capacitor banks, harmonic filters, SVGs and MV automatic power-factor panels, and this division grew about 78% in FY26 and 63% in Q1FY27 (AR FY26; press release, 7-Aug-2026). Its rupee size is not disclosed.

The inputs

FACT Copper, aluminium and polyethylene dominate input cost. Price escalation clauses are built into the longer EPC contracts, key inputs are tied up when large orders are bid, and a dedicated hedging desk has run since January 2022 (CARE, Feb-2026). MY INFERENCE The exposed piece is fixed-price product supply bid before a metal rally: the escalation protection is described for EPC, not for every drum.

The counterweight

  • Competition at EHV is not static. Larger listed cable makers also make EHV cable MY INFERENCE; not quantified in any Universal filing, and the industry is adding capacity across voltage classes.
  • The 400 kV licence has an end date. The NKT agreement runs to 8-Aug-2028 (AR FY26).
  • Export markets bring trade risk. The EU proposed an 8.7% provisional anti-dumping duty on Indian single-mode optical-fibre cable, including Universal's, in Jun-2024 (Reg 30 filing, 18-Jun-2024). Exports fell from ₹209 Cr in FY25 to ₹169 Cr in FY26 before jumping to ₹120 Cr in Q1FY27 alone as a 400 kV Middle-East order shipped (press releases).
  • Turnkey growth consumes cash (§5), and the capex budget has more than doubled since it was first set (§6).

Glossary

XLPE
Cross-linked polyethylene: polyethylene whose molecules are chemically tied together during curing, so it holds its shape when hot. The standard insulation for modern power cable.
LV / MV / HV / EHV
Low, medium, high and extra-high voltage. In Indian cable usage, roughly ≤1.1 kV, 3.3–33 kV, 66–132 kV and 220 kV and above.
VCV
Vertical continuous vulcanisation: curing the insulation in a vertical tower, used for thick EHV walls.
CCV
Catenary continuous vulcanisation: curing in a shallow curved tube; common for MV.
Degassing
Holding the cured cable in heated storage so that curing by-products escape before it is sheathed.
Prequalification (PQ) test
A long-duration test of a complete cable system (cable plus joints) required before a design can be used at EHV, under IEC 62067.
Route-km
Length of the trench or duct route. A three-phase circuit needs three cables, so cable-km can be three times route-km.
Turnkey / EPC
Engineering, procurement and construction: the supplier delivers a finished, tested link rather than drums of cable.
Retention money
Part of each bill the customer holds back until the project is accepted.
Equity accounting
Booking your share of an associate's profit and net worth without consolidating its revenue, costs or cash.
Associate / JV
An investee you significantly influence (associate) or jointly control with a partner (joint venture).
Preform
A solid glass rod with the fibre's refractive structure built in, which is heated and drawn into optical fibre.
Capacitor bank / SVG
Equipment that supplies reactive power and stabilises voltage; an SVG (static var generator) does it electronically.
HTLS conductor
High-temperature low-sag overhead conductor that carries more current on existing towers. Universal has a 2026 manufacturing agreement with TS Conductor Corp, USA.
