How the speciality films business actually works

An industry primer for Garware Hi-Tech Films — what the products physically are, how they are made, and who touches them between the plant and the car

This page explains the industry, not the investment case — valuation, conviction and risks stay in the profile. What follows is the mechanism: what a window film physically is (it is not one layer, it is seven), where a commodity plastic film stops and a speciality product begins, why raw-material prices barely move this company's cost, who stands between the factory and the person whose car gets wrapped, and why the same product sells at three very different margins depending on which of those people buys it.

1. The product — a window film is seven layers, not one

Almost everyone pictures window film as a sheet of tinted plastic. It is not. It is a laminate — a stack of very thin functional layers, each doing a different job, assembled on a coating line. The tint is often the least important layer in it.

That single fact explains why this business earns a 24% EBITDA margin while a plain plastic-film producer earns single digits: the value is in the layers you cannot see, and in the chemistry that puts them there.

2. Where these films actually go

Two end-markets do most of the work — a car and a building — and within each, Garware sells several different films to the same surface.

3. Chips to film — and the line where commodity ends

Polyester film starts life in an oil refinery. Naphtha and xylene become PTA and MEG; those become PET chips — small plastic pellets. The chips are melted, extruded as a thick sheet, then stretched in two directions at once, which is what makes the film strong, clear and dimensionally stable. The output is base film, and it is a commodity: hundreds of plants worldwide make it, and its price tracks crude.

Garware makes its own chips — a 66,000 MT/year plant — and then does not stop at base film. Everything after that point is coating, dyeing and lamination, and that is where the margin lives.

4. Deep-dyed vs chip-dyed — the technical fork in the road

When management is asked how Garware differs from the fifty-odd Chinese and Korean producers, the answer is not about price. It is about where the colour sits in the film — a distinction that sounds trivial and decides how the product ages.

"There are more than 50 producers in China and Korea who produce the products through chip dyed and glue dyed method, right? And whereas our products are deep dyed products. So the basic difference between us and them is very big."
— Deepak Joshi, Q4FY25 call, 7-May-2025

5. The transformation — nine years from commodity to speciality

This is the single most important chart about Garware, and it is not a growth chart. Revenue roughly two-and-a-half times over nine years is respectable but unremarkable. What changed is what the company sells: value-added speciality films went from a minority of the mix to 87% of revenue, and the margin went with it.

6. The channel — who touches the film between the plant and the car

Here is the part that makes Garware unusual, and it is a mirror image of the problem a component supplier has. Garware is not stuck deep in someone else's supply chain; it has been walking forward along its own — from selling anonymous rolls to distributors, to putting its brand on studios where a consumer's car is actually wrapped.

The economics of each step differ sharply, and management has quantified them.

7. The other quarter of the company — industrial films

Roughly 26% of revenue is the Industrial Product Division, and it is invisible to consumers because every one of its products ends up inside somebody else's product. It is lower-profile than PPF and it is where Garware's largest market share sits.

9. Geography and the tariff swing

About 75% of revenue is exported, to 90+ countries. That is the source of the premium franchise — and it is also why a policy decision in Washington can move a quarter more than anything happening in the plant.

10. The competitive map — premium, economy, and who is integrated

Management is careful about naming competitors, but it has described the structure of the market clearly enough to map it: four large players in the US market including Garware; a premium tier of American-made brands; and more than fifty producers in China and Korea operating in a segment Garware says it "hardly competes with."

12. What the company does not disclose · Glossary

Recorded as gaps rather than filled with inference:

  • Segment-level margins. Revenue splits 74/26 between consumer and industrial, but neither division's profitability is given — so the margin effect of mix can only be inferred, never checked.
  • A consistent PPF vs SCF revenue split. PPF's share appears only occasionally and usually in the context of tariffs (31% → 25%). There is no standing quarterly disclosure.
  • Volumes and realisation. Capacity is stated in lakh square feet, but quarterly volume and price per square foot are not — so it is not possible to separate growth from price.
  • The GHS store count is stale. Six were operational at the May-2026 call and the last figure carried in our profile is nine, against a 50-store FY27 target that was reconfirmed without a current count. Treat 9 as last-known, not current.
  • What the three new TPU products are. Asked directly, management declined on the call — "these things are beyond certain point, the strategy will become very public, which we can't say in the forum." Reasonable, and it means the FY28 margin bridge rests partly on undisclosed products.
  • The permanent CFO. An interim CFO was appointed in August 2026 and the word "CFO" does not appear once in the Q1FY27 transcript. Not an industry question, but it sits over every number on this page.
BOPET
Biaxially-oriented polyethylene terephthalate — polyester film stretched in two directions. The commodity base of this industry.
PET chips
The plastic pellets film is made from. Garware makes its own, 66,000 MT/year.
SCF
Sun control film — window film for cars and buildings; heat, glare, UV and privacy.
PPF
Paint protection film — a clear film applied over car paint to take stone chips and scratches.
TPU
Thermoplastic polyurethane — the flexible, self-healing body of a PPF. Garware's own line commissions Q3FY27.
VAP
Value-added products — the company's term for everything that is not commodity film. 87% of revenue.
CPD / IPD
Consumer Product Division (74%) and Industrial Product Division (26%).
LSF
Lakh square feet — the capacity unit used for films. 4,200 LSF = 420 million sq ft a year.
Deep-dyed
Colour driven into the film body, versus chip-dyed (colour in the pellets) or glue-dyed (colour in the adhesive).
Nano-dispersion
Garware's proprietary technology for dispersing infra-red-blocking particles through a coating.
Release liner
The silicone-coated backing peeled off and discarded when a film or label is applied.
Shrink film
Printed film that tightens onto a container when heated — the sleeve on a bottle. Garware holds ~70% of the Indian market.
GAS / GHS
Garware Application Studios (250+, automotive) and Garware Home Solutions (architectural retail, 50-store FY27 target).
CMVR
Central Motor Vehicles Rules — the Indian regulation automotive safety glazing film must comply with.
DGTR
Directorate General of Trade Remedies — India's antidumping authority, which has recommended a duty on Chinese TPU-based PPF.
D2C
Direct to consumer — selling through own studios rather than distributors. Carries 25–40% higher margin.
