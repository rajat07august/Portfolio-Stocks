Three industries, one company

An industry primer for Dynamatic Technologies — what an airframe Tier-1 actually delivers, what a gear pump does on a tractor, why a German foundry is a hard place to be, and how to read a P&L that mixes all three across three currencies
Built 31-Aug-2026 · Sourced from Dynamatic's Q1FY27 investor deck and results, six earlier quarterly decks, and the exchange filings on disk · Companion to Company Profiles/DYNAMATIC.md

This page explains the industries, not the investment case. Dynamatic is unusual on this watchlist because it is not in one business — it is in three, in three countries, with no shared customer, technology or cycle between them. What follows is what each one actually does, why one of them earns almost all of the profit, and the two reading habits you need before any of its numbers mean anything.

1. Three businesses that share almost nothing

Most diversified companies at least share a customer, a technology or a raw material. Dynamatic's three segments share none of those. They sell to different industries, on different cycles, in different currencies, using different processes.

2. 48% of revenue, 97% of profit

This is the single statistic that defines the company, and everything else on this page should be read against it.

3. What an airframe Tier-1 actually delivers

An aerostructures supplier does not sell parts by the kilo. It is given a work package — a defined piece of the aircraft — and is responsible for delivering it complete, to the aircraft maker's own drawings, in matched sets, on the OEM's build rate.

4. Why a single-source airframe position is durable

Dynamatic has been the single global source for the Airbus A320 flap track beam for roughly fifteen years. Understanding why an aircraft maker would allow that — and why it rarely changes its mind — is the whole moat argument for this segment.

5. The gear pump — the original business

Hydraulics is where Dynamatic started, and it is a genuinely strong industrial position that happens to sit in a slow-growing industry.

What the product does: when a tractor lifts a plough, a loader raises its bucket, or a tipper tilts its bed, a pump is converting engine rotation into pressurised oil. A gear pump is the simplest and most robust way to do that — two meshing gears in a tight housing, carrying oil around the outside and forcing it out under pressure. It is cheap, tolerant of dirt, and hard to break, which is exactly what farm equipment needs.

6. The German foundry, and why it is a hard place to be

Eisenwerk Erla, at Schwarzenberg in Germany, is a ferrous foundry — it melts iron and pours it into moulds to make intricate castings that have to survive extreme heat and vibration: turbocharger housings and exhaust manifolds, sitting inches from an engine's hottest gas.

It is described by the company as one of the most advanced ferrous foundries in Europe, capable of single-source work for BMW, Audi, Daimler, MAN and BorgWarner. It earned a 0.8% EBITDA margin in FY26. The gap between those two sentences is the whole section.

7. The currency mechanic — reading growth that isn't there

With plants in India, the UK and Germany, a majority of Dynamatic's revenue is earned in euros and pounds and reported in rupees. When the rupee weakens, reported revenue rises without a single extra part being made.

8. Three countries, three cost bases

Eight facilities across three countries is not just a currency exposure — it is three different labour rates, energy costs and regulatory regimes making broadly similar things. That creates a lever most single-country manufacturers do not have: work can be moved.

12. What is not disclosed

  • No earnings call since February 2024 — the largest gap in the file. There is no management Q&A, no guidance and no analyst challenge anywhere in the recent record. Every judgement rests on written disclosure that nobody has been able to question.
  • Order book. For an aerostructures business awarded multi-year work packages, no backlog figure is published — so forward visibility in the one segment that earns the profit cannot be measured.
  • Programme-level revenue. The A320 flap track beam and the A220 doors are described qualitatively; neither is sized, so the concentration inside Aerospace is unknown.
  • Customer concentration. Blue-chip names are listed for all three segments; no revenue share is given for any of them.
  • Capacity and utilisation at any of the eight facilities.
  • The Metallurgy plan, quantified. "Development of aerospace castings and forgings" has appeared for at least four quarters with no revenue, timeline or capex attached to it.
  • Dynauton Systems. The unmanned-systems arm and the Aerodata MoU carry no disclosed value, timeline or funding.

13. Glossary

Aerostructures
The physical structure of an aircraft — wings, fuselage sections, doors, control surfaces — as distinct from engines or avionics.
Tier-1 supplier
A supplier that sells complete assemblies directly to the aircraft maker, rather than parts to another supplier.
Work package
A defined portion of the aircraft that a supplier is responsible for delivering complete, to the OEM's drawings.
Ship-set
All the units of a given item needed for one aircraft — for the A220 doors programme, eight doors delivered as a matched set.
Flap track beam
The structural beam that carries the wing flap and its loads. Flight-critical, which is why single-sourcing it is a significant OEM decision.
AFT pylon
The rear structure connecting an engine to the wing.
Build-to-print
Manufacturing to the customer's design. The supplier owns the process capability, not the drawing.
Hydraulic gear pump
Two meshing gears in a close-fitting housing that convert engine rotation into pressurised oil flow — the standard way a tractor lifts an implement.
Ferrous foundry
A plant that melts iron and pours it into moulds to make castings. Extremely energy-intensive.
Turbocharger housing / exhaust manifold
Castings that sit against an engine's hottest exhaust gas — Erla's core products, and parts an electric vehicle does not have.
Constant currency
Growth recalculated at last year's exchange rates, stripping out translation. The number to use when a company earns in several currencies.
Segment EBITDA
Operating profit of one business unit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. For this company, the only margin figure worth reading.
