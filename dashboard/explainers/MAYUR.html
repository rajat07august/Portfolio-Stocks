How the synthetic leather business actually works

1. The product — what synthetic leather actually is

Synthetic leather is not plastic pretending to be leather in the way a toy is. It is an engineered textile: a woven or knitted fabric base, coated with a polymer, then embossed with a grain pattern and finished so it looks, feels and wears like hide. It is sold by the linear metre, and cut and sewn by whoever makes the seat, the shoe or the sofa.

The base fabric is what gives it tear strength and dimensional stability — the reason a seat cover survives ten years of people sliding across it. The coating is what gives it the look, the feel, the UV resistance and the cleanability. Both halves have to be right, and they are made by different processes.

2. Three chemistries, and why running all three matters

The coating can be one of three polymer families, and the choice sets the price, the feel and the customer. Most Indian competitors run one. Mayur runs all three — which is what lets it quote against any OEM's specification rather than only the ones its single line happens to suit.

3. How a roll gets made — and where the integration is

Two raw materials go in: yarn, which becomes the fabric, and paste PVC, which becomes the coating. Both are petroleum derivatives, which is why input costs move with crude and why management describes prices going "up very sharply, then a fall, then up again."

The step that separates Mayur from most of the Indian industry sits right at the start: it makes its own base fabric. The typical Indian competitor is a converter — it buys fabric in and only coats it.

4. Capacity is counted in metres per month

This is a business where the capacity number, the utilisation rate and the revenue potential can all be reconciled on the back of an envelope — and management does exactly that on the calls.

5. Six verticals, two very different halves

Mayur reports six product verticals. Read as one list they look like a diversified customer base; read as two halves — export and domestic — they explain nearly everything about the margin trend.

6. The value chain — from petroleum to a car seat

Mayur sits in the middle of a chain that starts at a refinery and ends on something you sit on. It does not sell to consumers, and mostly does not sell to the car maker directly either — the material goes to whoever cuts and sews the seat.

7. The qualification clock — the moat, and a flag on it

An automotive interior material cannot simply be sold to a car company. It has to pass the OEM's own testing — abrasion, tear, UV and heat ageing, fogging, odour, flammability, colour-fastness — and then be approved onto a specific vehicle platform. Once it is on that platform, it generally stays there for the platform's life, because re-testing an alternative costs the OEM time and money for no benefit.

That is the moat: not the coating line, but the certificate. It is also why a supplier's growth is lumpy and slow to arrive — you win a platform, then wait for it to launch.

8. Price versus volume — the most important line in the results

If you read one thing about Mayur's numbers, read this. Revenue grew 24.5% in Q1FY27. Volume grew about 2%. Everything else was price and mix.

9. Why exports are a margin lever — and the quarter that questioned it

Export business earns roughly 5–8 percentage points more margin than domestic, and it is invoiced in dollars, so a weaker rupee lifts realisation without any commercial negotiation. FY26 carried a ₹30cr forex gain on that mechanism. Export mix has gone from about 35% two years ago to roughly 42.5% of standalone revenue, guided to 40–45% for FY27.

Then Q1FY27 delivered a test of the theory, and an analyst put it plainly on the call: export OEM grew about 40% year on year — so why did gross margin not improve?

10. The tariff exposure and the plant that has not been decided

The strength and the risk are the same fact. About 42% of revenue is dollar-priced export, and the fastest-growing part of it goes to US automotive. A US tariff regime lands directly on that.

The industry answer is well understood — build a plant inside the tariff wall. Mayur has been discussing it for roughly two years and has not chosen a location.

12. What Mayur does not disclose

  • No investor presentation exists at all. Every number above comes from spoken commentary or a results filing. There is no slide-deck cross-check — the verification layer that every other company in this library provides is simply absent here.
  • Segment margins. The six verticals are given in revenue only. The 5–8pp export premium is described, never tabulated, so the mix-shift argument cannot be quantified from disclosure.
  • Customer concentration. OEM names are discussed qualitatively; no top-customer share is published for a business where export OEM is the growth engine.
  • PU capacity and utilisation — disclosed for PVC, not for PU, which is the chemistry the growth argument depends on.
  • Order book or platform pipeline. Nothing equivalent is reported, so a platform win becomes visible only when it turns into revenue.
  • Approval cycle length. Not stated by management on any call on disk — see the flag in §7.

13. Glossary

Synthetic leather
A fabric base coated with polymer and embossed to look like leather. Also called artificial leather, coated fabric or "uniquoter" in Mayur's own naming.
Base fabric
The knitted or woven textile underneath the coating; provides tear strength. Mayur makes its own; most Indian competitors buy it in.
Paste PVC
The PVC grade used for coating, supplied as a paste and a petroleum by-product. One of Mayur's two main raw materials, alongside yarn.
PU / semi-PU
Polyurethane coating (premium, softest) and a PVC base with a PU top coat (the middle grade).
Embossing
Pressing a grain pattern into the coated surface — what makes it look like hide.
Linear metre
The unit of sale. Capacity, output and price are all expressed per metre.
Export OEM
Material supplied to overseas vehicle programmes — Mayur's largest and fastest-growing vertical.
Replacement
The aftermarket: re-covering worn seats, outside the OEM channel.
Furnishing
Upholstery for furniture and interiors.
Platform
A specific vehicle model generation. Material is approved onto a platform and typically supplied for its production life.
RFQ
Request for quotation — the OEM's invitation to bid against a written material specification.
Approved vendor
Passed the customer's audit and testing. Not the same as having an order — Mayur's PU business sits exactly at that distinction.
