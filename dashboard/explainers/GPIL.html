How integrated steel actually works

An industry primer for Godawari Power & Ispat — what happens to iron at each step, why owning the mine is the whole margin, and what breaks when you cannot get into it
Built 30-Aug-2026 · Sourced from GPIL's 7 official transcripts, the Q1FY27 deck and results, and the exchange filings on disk · Companion to Company Profiles/GPIL.md

This page explains the industry, not the investment case. What follows is the mechanism: what physically happens to iron ore as it becomes a steel bar, why an integrated producer sells at every stage rather than only the last, what captive ore is actually worth per tonne, and why in this business the state government is a counterparty at three separate points of the chain.

1. The chain — from ore body to steel bar

Most steel companies occupy one or two links of a long chain and buy everything upstream of them. GPIL owns the whole thing: it mines the ore, agglomerates it into pellets, reduces pellets into sponge iron, melts sponge into billets, and rolls billets into bars and structural products — with its own power stations feeding the furnaces.

Each step is a separate plant with its own capacity, and the capacities do not match neatly. That mismatch is not sloppiness — it is the design, and §3 explains why.

2. What each step does to the iron

The chain in §1 is not five ways of moving the same material around. Each step changes what the iron physically is — and each change is why the next step is possible.

3. The chain leaks at every stage — and that is the business model

Here is the fact that reframes GPIL, and it is visible only if you put the production table next to the sales table. Every plant in the chain sells part of its output to the market instead of passing it down the line. The company is not a steel maker with a mine attached; it is a merchant of five different products that happen to sit in sequence.

4. What captive ore is worth

Everything about the integrated model comes down to one spread. In Q1FY27 GPIL's landed cost of its own ore was roughly ₹2,900 a tonne. Buying the same ore in the market cost roughly ₹6,200 a tonne. That gap — about ₹3,300 on every tonne it digs rather than buys — is the return on owning a mine.

5. Q1FY27 — the quarter that showed the leverage running backwards

Q1FY27 is the clearest lesson in this file, and it is a pure industry-structure story rather than a demand story. Revenue grew 32%. EBITDA grew 3%. Margin fell 543 basis points.

6. Three permissions, three different points of the chain

This is the part of the industry an outsider systematically underestimates. Owning every link means needing a permission for every link — and in FY27 three separate approvals went against GPIL at once, each stopping a different part of the business.

7. Why the beneficiation plant is moving to the mine

A small detail from the Q1FY27 call explains a ₹325cr project better than any strategy slide. Today GPIL trucks its entire mined ore — waste rock included — from the mine to the factory complex, and separates the useful fraction there. It is paying freight on material it is about to throw away.

"Beneficiation will mainly help us in reducing the mining cost, because right now I'm beneficiating the entire ore in factory complex by paying a transportation of INR1,000. So, by beneficiating in the mines, my mining cost will go down of usable concentrate, which will feed to the pellet plant."
— Abhishek Agrawal, Q1FY27 call, 10-Aug-2026

8. Captive power — the other input nobody talks about

Melting sponge iron and rolling billets are electricity-hungry operations, and in a commodity business the power tariff lands directly on the margin. GPIL's answer is to generate most of what it uses — including from heat it was previously throwing away.

9. The guidance scorecard — unusual disclosure, scored in public

Commodity producers rarely publish volume targets, because volumes are the one thing shareholders can check. GPIL publishes full-year volume guidance product by product, and then prints its own achievement percentage against it every quarter. A quarter is 25% of a year, so the pace line is easy to read.

10. Where the capital is actually going

A reader who stopped at §1 would assume the capex programme is about making more steel. It is not. The single largest line in the committed pipeline is a battery-storage plant.

12. What is not disclosed

  • Segment profitability — the big one. GPIL reports as a single segment under Ind AS 108. Volumes are published product by product, but there is no disclosure of where profit is earned along the chain. Whether the money comes from merchant pellets, from rolled products or from ferro alloys cannot be read from the accounts.
  • Internal transfer volumes and prices. The internal-consumption figures in §3 are production minus sales, computed here. The company does not publish what it transfers between plants, or at what price.
  • Realisation per tonne by product. Volumes are given; realisations are described qualitatively as "improved" rather than tabulated.
  • The captive-versus-market ore split in a given quarter. Management describes "higher market procurement" without quantifying what share of pellet feed was bought.
  • Cost build-up per tonne. No disclosure of ore, coal, power and conversion cost per tonne at any stage, which is what would let an outsider verify the ₹2,900/₹6,200 spread independently.
  • A dated path back for the idled pellet plant. The suspension notice states the cause; no restart date is given.

13. Glossary

Iron ore fines
Crushed ore, too powdery to feed directly into a furnace — gas cannot pass through a packed bed of dust.
Pellet
Ore fines rolled into marble-sized balls with a binder and fired hard, so a furnace can breathe through the charge. Changes the form, not the chemistry.
Beneficiation
Separating the useful iron-bearing fraction from waste rock. Doing it at the mine avoids paying freight on the waste.
Fe grade
Iron content of the ore. Ari Dongri runs at ~65% Fe, which is high — less waste travels with every tonne.
Sponge iron / DRI
Directly reduced iron: pellets or lump ore with the oxygen chemically stripped out using coal, without melting. Porous, hence "sponge".
Billet
A semi-finished square bar of cast steel — the stock that gets reheated and rolled into finished products.
Rolled products
TMT bars, rounds, wire and structural sections — the construction-facing end of the chain.
Ferro alloys
Iron-alloy additives used in steelmaking. At GPIL a separate saleable product line rather than a step in the chain.
WHRB
Waste Heat Recovery Boiler — captures hot exhaust from the sponge-iron kilns to raise steam and generate power from fuel already paid for.
Overburden
The rock and soil removed to reach the ore. It has to be dumped somewhere, which is why a land clearance can cap mining.
Merchant sales
Output sold to outside buyers rather than consumed in the company's own next process.
MnT
Million tonnes. GPIL states mining and pellet capacities in these units.
BESS
Battery Energy Storage System — grid-scale batteries. The largest single line in the current capex pipeline, and not a steel asset.
