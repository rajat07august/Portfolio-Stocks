How an integrated EPC contractor actually works

An industry primer for Sathlokhar Synergys — why a construction company can book record profits and still consume cash every single year, and why the one ratio a screener shows you for this business points exactly the wrong way
Built 9-Sep-2026 · Sourced from Sathlokhar's own transcripts, investor decks and audited filings on disk · Companion to Company Profiles/SATHLOKHAR.md

This page explains the industry, not the investment case. What follows is the mechanism: what an EPC contractor physically hands over, why doing five trades in-house rather than one changes the economics, what a pre-engineered building actually is and why the factories that make them have to be regional — and then the thing that governs this entire sector, which is that the moment you record revenue, the moment you are allowed to send a bill, and the moment cash arrives are three different dates that can sit years apart.

1. What this company actually hands over

Most industrial companies sell an object. An EPC contractor sells something stranger: a working building, on a date, for a fixed price. EPC stands for engineer, procure, construct — the contractor designs it, buys everything that goes into it, builds it, and hands over the keys.

Sathlokhar describes its own scope in one sentence on every call: “end-to-end turnkey construction services across design, civil construction, pre-engineered building construction, MEP systems, solar installation, interior works and finishes, utilities, and statutory approvals.” Q1FY27 transcript, opening remarks

The word doing the work there is turnkey. The client signs one contract and receives a finished factory. Every trade in between — the drawings, the government approvals, the foundations, the steel frame, the plumbing, the electrical distribution, the rooftop solar, the flooring — is the contractor's problem, not the client's.

The clients are industrial. Sathlokhar's named orders in a single recent quarter include Reliance Consumer Products (₹102.71cr, Kurnool), a Taiwan-based SEZ developer at Panapakkam (₹105.37cr plus ₹22.5cr), Carrier Airconditioning & Refrigeration (₹94.11cr including GST, at Sri City), TAFE, EDAC Engineering, Ceylon Beverages and Anabond. Q1FY27 transcript and 11-Aug-2026 filing

The one distinction to hold on to

A factory building is not a product the contractor makes and sells from stock. It is a contract the contractor performs over time — typically three to ten months for Sathlokhar. Q1FY27 investor deck Almost every accounting oddity in this sector, and everything in sections 8 to 10 below, follows from that single difference.

2. The value chain — one contract instead of nine

To see what “integrated” buys, look at what the client avoids. A company building a pharma plant has a choice. It can appoint nine specialists and manage the seams between them itself — and the seams are where construction projects fail, because when the electrical contractor cannot start until the civil contractor finishes and the civil contractor is late, nobody owns the delay. Or it can sign one contract and make the delay someone else's problem.

Sathlokhar's stated differentiator is exactly this: “our fully integrated execution model with dedicated in-house architecture, civil, PEB and MEP teams enabling better project control, faster execution timelines, cost efficiencies and superior delivery quality.” Q4FY26 transcript

There is a second, less obvious consequence of integration, and management states it as a cost fact rather than a quality one:

“Today, only around 30% to 35% of our operations demand a conventional civil labor while the majority of execution is driven by engineering and technical professionals and this model enhances scalability, execution speed, operational efficiency and margin stability.”
G. Thiyagu, Q4FY26 transcript

That matters because the two halves of construction scale very differently. Civil work is labour-limited — to build twice as much you need roughly twice as many people on site, and site labour is the hardest input in Indian construction to hire, supervise and retain. Engineered work is not. A steel frame is designed once, manufactured in a factory, and bolted together on site by a small crew. MY INFERENCE A contractor whose mix is two-thirds engineered rather than two-thirds civil is buying a growth path that does not run into a labour ceiling at the same point — which is the mechanism behind the phrase “enhances scalability”.

3. The 35 / 35 / 30 split, and why it explains the whole strategy

Asked how a rupee of project value divides, management gave a precise answer:

“In our business model, as I mentioned, overall, 100%, 35%, 35%, and balance is 30% for utilities and MEP works. So, 35% contributing from PEB sector.”
G. Thiyagu, Q4FY26 transcript

So a typical Sathlokhar project is roughly 35% civil, 35% pre-engineered building, 30% MEP and utilities. Read that beside the previous section and the strategy assembles itself.

This is the whole logic of the PEB factory. A third of every project's value is steel structure that Sathlokhar designs and sells but does not manufacture — it buys steel, ships it to a job-worker, pays a processing fee, and takes the fabricated frame back. Backward integration into PEB is therefore not a diversification; it is the company buying back the biggest line of its own bill of materials.

Management sizes the gain modestly and is careful about what it is really for:

“For the whole financial [year], we are trying to improve 1% on the bottom line.” … and separately, that the point is “more than profit, it's our own facility where we are getting faster in construction.”
Q4FY26 and Q1FY27 transcripts

What the PEB plant is not, yet

The facility is intended to run as a separate vertical selling to outside customers too — “all the other competitor also take the PEB from us” — with a stated ambition of ₹200cr-plus of turnover from it. But management explicitly cautioned against modelling third-party sales yet: “as of now, our own requirements are huge.” Q1FY27 transcript The first plant's inauguration was fixed for 30-Aug-2026, with commercial production about 60 working days later, after consent-to-operate from the pollution control board, a high-tension power connection, a fire licence and machine trial runs — management's own sequence, which it expected to yield roughly five months of output in FY27.

4. What a pre-engineered building is

“PEB” appears fifty-seven times in a single Sathlokhar earnings call, so it is worth knowing exactly what the thing is.

A pre-engineered building is a steel-framed structure whose every member is designed, cut, welded, drilled and painted in a factory, then trucked to site and bolted together. Nothing is fabricated at the site. The alternative — reinforced cement concrete, or RCC — is the opposite: steel reinforcement bars are tied into cages on site, timber or steel shuttering is built around them, concrete is poured in, and then everyone waits while it cures.

For the buildings Sathlokhar sells — warehouses, industrial sheds, factories — the clear span matters commercially, not just structurally. A warehouse with columns down the middle is a worse warehouse: it constrains racking layout and forklift movement. The company's own deck describes the products as “high capacity warehouses designed for efficient storage, logistics and material handling” and “steel and prefabricated sheds for manufacturing units, workshops and storage facilities.” Q1FY27 investor deck

5. Why PEB factories have to be regional

Sathlokhar's stated ambition is five to six PEB plants across India, roughly one a year, with Odisha named as the next location after Tamil Nadu. For most manufacturers that would be a strange plan — one large efficient plant usually beats six small ones. Management gives the reason in a single phrase:

“Every year we would like to have one PEB facility in a different location wherein it serves the purpose of transportation cut and also to facilitate the customers to get a faster PEB buildings to the global standards.”
G. Thiyagu, Q1FY27 transcript

MY INFERENCE The economics behind that phrase are worth spelling out, because they are what makes PEB a regional business everywhere in the world. A fabricated steel frame is bulky relative to its value. A truck carrying tapered columns and rafters fills its volume long before it reaches its weight limit, so freight is charged on space, not tonnage, and the cost climbs with every kilometre. Beyond some radius, the freight bill overwhelms whatever the plant saved in fabrication — and a competitor with a plant nearer the site wins on landed cost without being any better at making steel.

6. How an order is born

An EPC contractor does not have a sales pipeline in the ordinary sense. It has a bid pipeline — the total value of tenders and proposals it has actually submitted and is waiting to hear back on. Sathlokhar's stood at ₹22,106cr at the Q1FY27 call and ₹22,155cr ten days later. Q1FY27 transcript; 11-Aug-2026 filing

Against that, its own deck states a historical bid win ratio of approximately 10% to 15%, and management deliberately plans on the bottom of that band:

“Our conservative numbers what we are aiming is minimum 10% success, it comes to ₹2,200 [crore].”
G. Thiyagu, Q1FY27 transcript

Two features of this funnel are worth noticing because they are unusual.

First, the pipeline is twenty-seven times the annual revenue. That is not a claim about demand so much as a statement about how the industry works: bidding is cheap relative to building, so a contractor bids on far more than it can possibly execute and lets the win ratio do the selection. The constraint on a firm like this is not finding work — it is capacity and capital to execute.

Second, much of the work is repeat. The deck puts it plainly: “repeat work from existing clients is how this business grows.” Reliance Consumer Products has been a client for “last 3-plus years… consistently booking every year a good order”, and in the most recent quarter one Taiwanese SEZ developer placed both a Phase 3 order and a Phase 2 top-up. Q1FY27 deck and transcript MY INFERENCE Repeat orders from a satisfied client skip most of the funnel — which is why a win ratio measured across all bids understates the odds on the bids that matter most.

7. Inside a project — and why revenue is back-loaded

Management describes the stages of a job in a fixed order, and the order is the key to the seasonality:

“As projects progress through engineering, procurement, mobilization, and execution stages, revenue recognition is expected to remain stronger during the second half of the financial year consistent with the nature of our business.”
G. Thiyagu, Q1FY27 transcript

Management confirmed the sequencing consequence directly: “when we execute civil and PEB, the further other works like MEP, electrical, those things would be carryforward to next financial year.” Q1FY27 transcript A single order therefore pays out across two financial years — civil and steel first, services later.

Stack enough projects started at different times and a pattern emerges in the quarterly revenue: June quarters are the weakest, March quarters the strongest.

8. Reading the numbers I — revenue, billing and cash are three different dates

This is the section that matters. Everything above is context for it.

When a contractor builds a factory over ten months, when has it earned the money? Accounting's answer — and it is the right answer — is as the work is done. This is the percentage-of-completion principle: if a contractor has completed 40% of a ₹100cr contract, it recognises ₹40cr of revenue and the associated profit, whether or not it has sent an invoice and whether or not anyone has paid.

But the contract does not let it invoice whenever it likes. Billing happens at milestones written into the contract — foundations complete, structure erected, services commissioned — and each bill must usually be certified by the client's engineer before it is payable. And even once paid, the client keeps back a slice, the retention, until the defect liability period expires long after handover.

So money moves through four stages, and each transition is a delay.

The question that was asked and not answered

On the Q1FY27 call an analyst asked for the one number that would show whether the gap in stage 2 was widening or closing:

“So, out of that if you can share the figure for 30th June, 2026, if you have it handy?”

“Yes, sir. As of now as I mentioned, I'm right now at the Delhi airport… Maybe once you mail us, we will come back to you, sir.”

MY INFERENCE The current unbilled balance is the single most informative disclosure this business could make, and at the time of the call it was not on the record. Anyone tracking this industry should treat the unbilled line — not the order book, and not the revenue growth — as the number to ask for.

9. Reading the numbers II — the ratio that points backwards

Here is where a stock screener will actively mislead you about an EPC contractor, and it is worth understanding the mechanism because it applies to the whole sector.

Debtor days — trade receivables divided by revenue, times 365 — is the standard measure of how fast a company collects. For Sathlokhar it collapsed between FY25 and FY26, from about 117 days to 34 days. On any screen that reads as one of the great working-capital improvements you will see in a small-cap industrial.

It is not an improvement. Nothing was collected. Trade receivables fell because the claim on the customer moved out of “trade receivables” and into a different line of the balance sheet.

A caution on the FY25 base itself

Sathlokhar's FY26 statements are labelled “Restated” and carry an opening column at 1-Apr-2024, and the FY25 revenue figure differs between sources — the company's own deck shows ₹369.69cr while Screener carries ₹399cr. Any ratio with FY25 in the denominator inherits that ambiguity. The figures above use the company's own number and say so; the profile documents the discrepancy in full.

10. Why growth itself consumes the cash

Put sections 8 and 9 together and the structural fact of this industry appears: a fast-growing EPC contractor consumes cash as a matter of arithmetic, not mismanagement. Every new project needs design, materials and mobilisation paid for up front, and adds to the pile of work done but not yet billable. Double the revenue and you roughly double that pile — while the profit on last year's smaller pile is still working its way to the bank.

Sathlokhar's FY26 cash-flow statement is a clean worked example of the mechanism.

That was not a one-year event. Across FY25 and FY26 together, Sathlokhar reported ₹159cr of profit before tax and consumed ₹259cr of operating cash. The gap was funded externally — in FY26, ₹90.44cr of fresh equity and ₹80.70cr of short-term borrowing. Audited FY26 cash-flow statement

Which is why the financing side of an EPC contractor's balance sheet is part of the operating story, not a separate topic. Sathlokhar's own description of its bank lines shows the structure:

“Long term overall around ₹250 crores overall bankers we have got the sanction and out of that 70-30, 30% for bank guarantee and 70% for working capital limit. And at this juncture, we have almost utilized 70% of the money being taken for the cash flows.” — with the CFO adding: “Around 180, sir.”
G. Thiyagu and P. Vijaykumar, Q1FY27 transcript

MY INFERENCE The 30% earmarked for bank guarantees is not idle. Construction clients require performance guarantees and advance-payment guarantees before work starts, so a contractor must have guarantee capacity simply to accept an order — which means a contractor's growth is capped by its banking limits in two directions at once: it needs guarantee capacity to win the work and working-capital capacity to fund it.

11. Steel, cement, and who carries the price

Two inputs dominate. Management is explicit about which:

“The major portion is PEB, I mean steel and cement. In any project when it comes to PEB or RCC structures, steel and cement is the primary factors. So, we are protecting these two materials.”
G. Thiyagu, Q1FY27 transcript

A fixed-price contract signed today and built over ten months carries an obvious hazard: if steel rises 15% in month four, the contractor absorbs it. The industry's answer is the price escalation clause — the contract names a base price for key materials on the day of signing, and movements away from that base are passed to the client.

Sathlokhar adopted this explicitly and recently:

“Post March 2026 onwards we have been started mentioning the base price, also protecting the current market price. Both we have been addressed to the clients… all our projects we are protecting the base price including the material requirements on time.”
Asked directly whether it is therefore a pass-through, including steel for PEB: “Yes.”
Q1FY27 transcript

What a pass-through does and does not do

It protects the margin, not the revenue line. When steel prices rise, a pass-through contractor's revenue rises with them without any more building being done — and when they fall, revenue falls while activity is unchanged. MY INFERENCE Reported revenue growth in this sector is therefore part volume and part steel price, and the two cannot be separated from the outside unless management gives a volume number. Sathlokhar does not publish one.

It also cuts the other way on margin. Management attributed part of a 418 basis point EBITDA margin improvement in Q1FY27 to “lower material cost as a percentage of total income”, and separately noted that “steel prices have moderated from the elevated levels witnessed earlier this year.” Q1FY27 transcript MY INFERENCE A margin helped by falling input prices is not the same thing as a structurally better cost base, and the two look identical in a single quarter's percentage.

If you remember one thing

In an EPC business, the order book tells you about the future, the P&L tells you about the work, and only the cash-flow statement tells you whether the work is turning into money. Growth, profit and cash can point in three different directions for years at a time without anything being wrong — and the number that reconciles them is the unbilled balance.

13. What is not disclosed · Glossary · Sources

What is not disclosed

Stated as gaps rather than passed over, because each one limits what can be concluded from the outside:

  • The unbilled revenue balance is not published by the company. The ₹234cr figure used throughout this page came from an analyst's question and was not confirmed on the record. The 30-Jun-2026 balance was asked for on the call and deferred to email.
  • There is no segment P&L. The 35 / 35 / 30 split is management's description of a typical project, not an audited segment disclosure, so margins by scope line cannot be derived.
  • No volume or physical-output measure is published — no square feet built, no tonnes of steel erected. With a steel and cement pass-through in the contracts, that makes it impossible to separate price from activity in the revenue line.
  • Order-book composition by client is partial. Large orders are announced individually, but there is no periodic disclosure of client concentration.
  • The PEB plant's capacity is not quantified in tonnes. The “₹200cr-plus turnover” ambition is a revenue aspiration, not a rated capacity, and management cautioned against modelling third-party sales at all for now.
  • FY25 comparatives are restated and differ across sources (₹369.69cr per the company, ₹399cr per Screener), so every FY26 growth rate depends on which base is used.

Glossary

EPC
Engineer, Procure, Construct. A contractor that designs the facility, buys everything for it and builds it — handing over a finished, working asset.
Turnkey
A contract where the client's only obligation is to pay and take the keys. All coordination risk sits with the contractor.
PEB
Pre-Engineered Building. A steel structure whose members are designed, cut, welded and drilled in a factory, then bolted together on site. Contrasted with RCC.
RCC
Reinforced Cement Concrete. Steel bars tied and concrete poured in place at the site, requiring curing time before work continues.
MEP
Mechanical, Electrical and Plumbing — a building's services: power distribution, HVAC, water, drainage, fire systems. Roughly 30% of scope here, and installed last.
Clear span
A roof carried without internal columns. Commercially valuable in warehousing, where columns obstruct racking and material handling.
Percentage of completion
The accounting principle that revenue and profit on a long contract are recognised as the work is performed, not when it is invoiced or paid.
Unbilled revenue
Also contract asset. Work performed and recognised as revenue, but not yet billable because the contractual milestone has not been reached. Sits in current assets, not in trade receivables.
Retention money
A percentage of each bill the client withholds until the defect liability period after handover expires. Earned, invoiced, but deliberately unpaid for months or years.
Defect liability period
The window after handover during which the contractor must fix defects at its own cost. Retention is the client's security for it.
Bid pipeline
The total value of tenders and proposals submitted and awaiting an outcome. Not an order book — nothing in it is won.
Order book
Signed, confirmed work in hand. The executable order book nets off what has already been billed.
Bank guarantee (BG)
A bank's undertaking to pay the client if the contractor fails to perform. Required before many contracts can even be accepted, and it consumes a bank limit.
Price escalation clause
A contract term naming a base price for key materials, with movements away from it passed to the client. Protects margin, but makes revenue move with input prices.
Class 1A PWD
The highest contractor registration category with the Public Works Department. It confers eligibility to bid for large government work — not revenue.
