Where a triazole maker sits

An industry primer for Astec LifeSciences: what an agrochemical "technical" physically is, why the same plant runs two businesses with two completely different price systems, how Chinese supply sets the price of one of them, and why the quarterly numbers (the CDMO line, the breakeven quarter, the cash flow) each mislead in their own way

This page explains the industry, not the investment case. It has no valuation, no conviction and no target; those live in the profile. What follows is the mechanism: what Astec makes, who buys it and on what terms, where the price comes from, what it costs to run a multi-step chemistry plant, and how to read the three numbers in its recent results that are easiest to misread.

1. The product, physically: a pure molecule in a drum

A farmer never sees what Astec makes. A bottle of fungicide on a shop shelf is a formulation: a small amount of active chemical dissolved or suspended in solvents, wetting agents and water, sold under a brand. The active chemical inside it is the technical (short for technical-grade active ingredient). It is a single, highly purified molecule, shipped by the drum or bag to whoever will formulate and brand it. Astec makes the technical, and the intermediates that go into technicals. Its own description is "agrochemical active ingredients (technical), bulk, formulations and intermediate products", and its customers are businesses, not farmers. Press releases, "About Astec", 2025–26; FY26 AR BRSR.

Its home ground is one family of molecules, the triazole fungicides. Two are named in its calls: tebuconazole and propiconazole, each made on "dedicated lines… we don't interchange them" (Jul-2021). In 2021 management said it made "about 6 or 7" of the triazoles in a global triazole market of about "$3.3 billion", with most revenue from "about 3 or 4", and put its share at under 5%.

Industry background Triazoles stop a fungus from building its cell membrane, and they work on a very wide range of crops and diseases. Management's own explanation of why the class has lasted is the useful one. Newer fungicide classes (strobilurins, then SDHIs) were each expected to replace triazoles, but fungi grew resistant to the newcomers when used alone, so "they can only be used effectively when they are combined with a triazole… the usage of triazoles it is like a bread and butter" (Jan-2021). That is a stable product. It also makes it a widely made one, which is what §5 is about.

Alongside the triazoles, Astec makes herbicides and herbicide intermediates, mostly for other companies' patented molecules, in a dedicated plant with "high-potency" containment. That second line of work is the contract (CDMO) business in §3.

2. The chain: from a Chinese starting material to a farmer's spray tank

Two facts about the input side carry through the rest of the page.

  • Much of the input still comes from China. In May-2022 management put China at "roughly… 65% to 67%" of raw-material spend. In May-2026 the COO said "around 47% of the total imports have come from China." The two figures are measured differently and cannot be compared directly, but both are large. The supplier of the inputs is also the biggest competitor for the output, which is a structural bind for every Indian triazole maker.
  • Backward integration is a switch, not a wall. Management describes it as an option: "for most of our key products, we are fully backward integrated… we have flexibility and ability to start in production of some of the input raw materials within a few weeks" (Oct-2022), and "if there is a supply constraint or the prices goes up… we can immediately start" (May-2026). MY INFERENCE That protects supply in a squeeze. It does not protect price, because it only makes sense when making the input is cheaper than buying it.

3. One plant, two businesses, two price systems

Astec reports a single segment, "Agrochemicals", but its press releases split revenue in two, and the split is the key to the whole business. The same reactors serve two kinds of customer who pay in opposite ways.

Why would an innovator hand its molecule to Astec at all? Management's 2021 answer is the clearest description of the CDMO business on record: innovators "want to bring in their new molecules into their plants and they don't invest in new capital expenditure. So, the only way to bring in a new molecule is to remove an old product out… the innovators are coming to us and saying they'll make this for us" (Jul-2021). CDMO work at Astec is, largely, an innovator's older or overflow chemistry, handed out to free its own capacity. Add a "China+1" wish to have a second source outside China, and that is the demand.

4. The ₹100 cost stack: a chemistry plant with a heavy fixed load

The FY26 consolidated profit-and-loss account shows what it costs to run eight multipurpose plants. Two features dominate: the materials are only two-thirds of revenue, which leaves a real conversion margin, and most of that margin is consumed by costs that do not fall when output falls.

Three things follow.

  • This is real chemistry. Power and fuel at 8.5% of revenue, and a separate effluent-treatment bill, are the signature of a plant that heats, cools, reacts and cleans in several steps. The new herbicide block is a "zero-liquid discharge" facility (FY26 AR). The value added between input and output is substantial, which is why gross margin was ~40% at the FY22 peak.
  • The fixed load is heavy. Employees (₹14.0), depreciation (₹10.0) and interest (₹7.7) take ₹31.8 of every ₹100 before anything else, and much of the power bill behaves like a fixed cost when a multipurpose plant is running a campaign. So a small change in gross margin moves profit a great deal, in both directions. §10 puts numbers on how far.
  • Depreciation and interest are the price of the build-out. The R&D centre (~₹110–120 Cr, per May-2022), the herbicide plant and a ₹300–350 Cr capex year in FY23 were started at the FY22 peak, and they carry depreciation of ₹44.9 Cr and interest of ₹34.5 Cr through the trough. May-2022 and Oct-2022 transcripts; FY26 AR Notes 32–33.

5. The enterprise price is made in China

An off-patent triazole is the same molecule whoever makes it, and China makes most of the world's supply. So Astec's enterprise price is set by how much Chinese capacity is running and how much stock distributors are already holding. The five earnings calls record the mechanism happening in real time, in both directions.

Management's own description of how it runs this business is candid about how much of it is timing: "there will be quarters wherein we might be taking 8 out of 10 calls right on strategic sourcing and we will have good profitability. There could be a scenario wherein the calls do not go as we expect and that will impact the margin" (May-2022). Buying inputs at the right moment is a large part of what the enterprise business earns, and it is exactly the part a quarterly margin cannot separate from underlying cost.

Two further features of this price:

  • Channel stock. When prices rise, buyers stock up ahead of shortages. When the shortage ends, they stop buying until the stock is used. The Oct-2022 call describes precisely that, "a lot of these buyers in the market have topped up in anticipation of the supply shortage which has led to some inventory buildup", and it was the start of a destocking phase that the FY26 AR still describes as "elevated channel inventories… delayed order replenishment."
  • Regulation. A European ban on one molecule can create oversupply everywhere else. In May-2022 management warned that "one of the products in European markets may go off this year… there will be oversupply situation and hence the pressure on prices."

6. The chemistry ladder: what the plants can make, and what they are being taught

A multipurpose plant is a set of reactors, distillation columns, filters and dryers that can be re-piped for a different sequence of steps. What limits it is not the hardware but the chemistries the team can run safely at scale. Management laid out the sequence it intended to climb in Oct-2022, and the FY26 AR shows where the R&D centre now is.

The plants that climb this ladder were sized with a rule of thumb management repeated on three calls: an asset turn of about 1.5–1.7x at full use, reached in the third or fourth year ("30%, 70%, 100% utilization", May-2022). On that basis the herbicide plant was expected to reach "Rs.230 Crores to Rs.270 Crores" of revenue at full use. Two caveats from management itself: utilisation of a multipurpose plant cannot be stated as one number ("getting to firm utilization rates is not at all possible," May-2022), and no figure for the herbicide plant's actual revenue has been published since.

7. 🔴 Reading the numbers (I): a CDMO quarter is a campaign, not a trend

The CDMO line is the one most often quoted from Astec's results, and on a quarterly basis it is close to meaningless. An innovator orders a campaign: a batch of a given molecule, made over some weeks and shipped when the customer's season needs it. A handful of campaigns landing in one quarter rather than the next swings reported CDMO revenue by more than half.

Two readings follow.

  • Q1FY27's "CDMO −42%" and the parent's "CDMO-led growth" are both about timing. The parent said on 4-May-2026 that it expected "a clear focus on CDMO-led growth". The next quarter's CDMO line fell 42%, which the company attributed to "a high base." On a rolling year, CDMO has sat between ₹196 Cr and ₹235 Cr for six quarters. Neither the fall nor the growth claim can be tested on one quarter.
  • The whole business is seasonal, too. Revenue ranged from ₹74 Cr (Q2FY26) to ₹159 Cr (Q4FY26) within one year. Management said as much in 2021: "due to the seasonality involved in our business, full year financials are a better representative." MY INFERENCE Q4 (Jan–Mar) is when stock ships for the northern-hemisphere spring spray season, which fits 62% exports, but the company has never broken its seasonality down.

8. 🔴 Reading the numbers (II): Q1FY27's breakeven went partly onto the shelf

Q1FY27 was reported as EBITDA breakeven on 8% lower revenue, "supported by favourable product mix, improved margins in both the Enterprise and Contract Manufacturing segments and enhanced capacity utilization at its plants." All of that may be true. The filed profit-and-loss lines add something the press release does not say: the plants made far more than they sold.

This matters because of §4. In a plant where ₹30-odd of every ₹100 is fixed, running full and building stock spreads the fixed cost over more output and parks part of it in inventory. The margin that shows up is real only when that stock is sold at a price that covers it. Q2FY27 (Jul–Sep) will show whether the ₹33.7 Cr moves out at those margins. The pattern of the past two years is a caution: finished goods fell from ₹112 Cr (Mar-24) to ₹25 Cr (Mar-26) as old stock was worked off, and FY25 carried a ₹20.2 Cr write-down when stock could not be sold at its cost.

9. 🔴 Reading the numbers (III): EBITDA breakeven, ₹81 Cr cash out

FY26 was the year Astec reached "EBITDA breakeven" (₹0.54 Cr, including ₹5.07 Cr of other income). It was also a year in which the business used ₹81 Cr of operating cash. Both are true, and the gap is almost entirely one balance-sheet line.

The general rule for this business: judge the cash on a full year, and look at the receivable days next to it. Screener's debtor days read 90 (FY23), 135 (FY24), 141 (FY25) and 202 (FY26). A rise that large can be seasonal timing, slower-paying customers, or longer terms offered to win volume, and the filings do not say which. In 2021 management described paying its own suppliers earlier to secure inputs ("we have reduced our payment terms with most of the vendors and we are paying them earlier", Oct-2021). A business that pays its suppliers early and collects from its customers late funds the difference with debt.

10. The counterweight: the fixed-cost wall

§4 showed the fixed load. This section asks the mechanical question it raises: at FY26's prices and cost structure, how big would the business have to be to stop losing money?

Three structural pressures sit behind the wall.

  • The triazole franchise is mature. Management put triazole growth at "3% to 4%" a year (May-2022). A shareholder's summary from 2026 puts it plainly: triazole chemistry is "more maturing and not very niche" (ValuePickr #161, not a company statement). Price, not volume, is what moves this half of the business.
  • The input supplier is the output competitor (§2). Every Chinese capacity restart shows up twice: as cheaper inputs and as cheaper competing product. MY INFERENCE In a downturn the second effect has dominated, as FY24–FY25 showed.
  • The CDMO book is concentrated. The FY26 AR lists "customer concentration risks in CDMO" among its concerns. Campaign timing (§7) is the visible symptom, and losing one programme would be the serious version. The number of CDMO customers and products has not been published. Management's 2022 goal was "at least two" new CDMO products a year.

12. What is not disclosed

  • 🔴 Any management explanation since Oct-2022. No earnings call has been held in nearly four years. The FY24–FY25 losses, the recovery, the Q1FY27 stock build and the receivables jump have been explained only in press-release sentences or not at all.
  • Volumes and prices by product. Management declined to name molecules or prices even when calls were held ("commercially classified information," Jul-2021). So the price and volume split of the enterprise business cannot be made.
  • CDMO customers, products and margins. The count of commercial CDMO products, customer concentration and the CDMO margin premium are unpublished. The only premium figures are "5–7%" (May-2022 call) and "8–10%" (a shareholder's AGM notes).
  • Capacity and utilisation. Not stated in any form. Management said it cannot be for multipurpose plants.
  • The herbicide plant's revenue against its ₹230–270 Cr full-use target, and whether the sulfonylurea, pyrimidine and fluorination work (§6) has produced any commercial product.
  • Export share, consistently. The FY26 AR says exports were 62% of revenue (₹276.2 Cr of ₹445.7 Cr, Note 27). On the parent's call the COO said "this year we clocked around 53% of the total revenue through exports." They cannot both be the same measure.
  • The ₹41.1 Cr GST show-cause notice (received 31-Jul-2025): the authority claims export refunds for Apr-2019 → Mar-2022 were wrongly paid because inputs were imported duty-free under Advance Authorisation. The company says the claim is "not maintainable" and will challenge it. No outcome has been filed.
  • The cause of the receivables rise (§9): timing, customer mix or credit terms.
  • Why the stock was built in Q1FY27 (§8), and at what cost it is carried.

13. Glossary · Sources

Glossary

Technical (AI)
Technical-grade active ingredient: the purified chemical that does the work in a pesticide, before it is formulated and branded. What Astec sells.
Formulation
The product a farmer buys: technical mixed with solvents, carriers and additives so it can be diluted and sprayed.
Intermediate
A molecule part-way along a synthesis route, sold to someone who will finish it.
Key starting material (KSM)
The basic chemicals a synthesis begins from. Largely imported, much of it from China.
Triazole
A class of fungicides (tebuconazole, propiconazole and others) that block a fungus's membrane building. Mature, widely used, and usually paired with newer classes to slow resistance.
Strobilurin / SDHI
Newer fungicide classes. Management's point is that each is typically used with a triazole, not instead of one.
Enterprise
Astec's term for selling its own off-patent technicals at market prices to formulators and distributors.
Registration / registered source
Regulatory approval to sell a pesticide in a country, which names the manufacturing source of the technical. Changing source means re-registering, which makes an approved source sticky.
CDMO / CMO / CRAMS
Contract (Development and) Manufacturing: making a molecule to another company's specification and process, usually an innovator's patented or proprietary product. Priced cost-plus.
Innovator
A company that discovers and patents new crop-protection molecules (the large global agrochemical firms).
Campaign
A production run of one molecule in a multipurpose plant, sized to an order, before the plant is cleaned and switched to the next product.
Multipurpose plant (MPP)
A set of reactors and separation equipment that can run different synthesis sequences in turn. Flexible, but has no single "capacity".
High-potency block
A contained production area for very active molecules that need extra protection for workers and the environment.
Channel inventory / destocking
Stock held by distributors and formulators. When it is high, they stop ordering until it is used up, even if farmer demand is steady.
Net realisable value (NRV)
What stock can be sold for, less selling cost. Stock carried above it must be written down.
Advance Authorisation
An Indian scheme allowing duty-free import of inputs used to make exports.
China+1
Buyers' wish to have a second source outside China for critical molecules.
