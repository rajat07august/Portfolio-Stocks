1. The product — what "aero tooling" physically is

Start with the object, because almost every misunderstanding of this business starts with imagining the wrong one. Unimech does not make jet engines, engine parts, or anything that leaves the ground. It makes the steel equipment that surrounds an engine while people work on it.

A modern turbofan weighs two to three tonnes, is assembled from thousands of parts to tolerances measured in microns, and cannot be laid on a bench. To build one — or to pull one apart for overhaul — a factory needs a stand to hold it, a cradle to carry it, a sling and beam to lift it, fixtures to rotate modules into position, jigs to check alignment, carts to move sub-assemblies, and a certified container to ship the finished engine to the airframer. Every one of those items is bespoke to a specific engine programme, and each is designed, machined, inspected and certified as carefully as a flying part, because a stand that fails while holding a ₹40cr engine is a catastrophe.

Unimech splits its tooling into aero-engine tools (for building and overhauling the engine), airframe tools (for assembling the aircraft structure — it names Airbus and Boeing programmes), MRO tools (the same kit, sold to overhaul shops), and ground support equipment. Named programmes on the company's own slide: LEAP, Pratt & Whitney, Rolls-Royce engines, and airframe tools for Airbus and Boeing. 2. The value chain — who pays whom, and where Unimech sits

The money in aerospace starts with an airline ordering aircraft. It reaches Unimech four steps later, and the shape of those four steps explains more about the company's quarterly results than any operating metric does.

The step most investors miss is the tool licensee. An engine OEM does not usually manufacture and sell its own tooling. It licenses the tool designs to Tier-1 companies who make and supply them to whoever is building or overhauling that engine. Unimech sits below those licensees. Management said this plainly when asked whether it deals directly with the LEAP engine OEM:

"On the tooling side, it's the licensees who basically, for specifically LEAP engine that you are talking about, licensees are the ones with whom Unimech works… Unimech works in the Tier-2 side where LEAP engine OEM is basically OEM and then there's a Tier-1. And typically, we work with the Tier-1. And in the tooling business, that's largely PO to PO business."
— Rajanikanth Balaraman, Q1FY27 call, 4-Aug-2026

4. The qualification funnel — how you become a supplier, and why it is the moat

You cannot simply quote for aerospace tooling. Before a customer will buy anything, the plant must hold the right certifications (AS9100D for aerospace quality systems, NADCAP for special processes like heat treatment and coating), and then each individual part must be qualified on its own through a First Article Inspection — you make one, and submit it with a dossier proving every dimension, material certificate and process step. The customer audits it. Only then does that part number become a qualified SKU you are allowed to supply.

Two ways the work arrives, and they are worth telling apart because they carry different economics:

  • Build-to-print — the customer supplies the drawing; Unimech manufactures to it. Lower engineering content, faster qualification, more substitutable.
  • Build-to-spec — the customer supplies a functional requirement; Unimech designs the tool and owns the engineering. Higher content, stickier, and the reason "advanced design, simulation and process engineering" appears first on the company's capability slide. Q1FY27 deck, capabilities panel.

5. Inside the plant — where a 65% gross margin comes from

The physical process is not exotic: cut metal, treat it, assemble it, test it, certify it, ship it. What is unusual is the cost structure that comes out the other end. Raw material and conversion is only about a third of revenue, and outsourced work is 3%. Almost everything else is people, machine time and paperwork — which is exactly what a customer is paying for when it buys a certified aerospace tool.

6. The three revenue engines — and the one-off → recurring shift

Unimech now has three businesses that sell different things to different people on different order rhythms. The important thing is not their relative size today; it is where each one sits on the line between "bought once" and "bought under contract."

7. Bellows, explained — what ₹450cr actually bought

Unimech spent essentially its whole IPO treasury on a company most investors have never heard of, making a product most have never seen. It is worth understanding the object, because the product explains the margin.

A metallic bellows is a flexible section of pipe, made of thin corrugated metal, that connects two things which move relative to each other. On an engine exhaust, one end is bolted to an engine that vibrates and gets very hot (metal grows when heated); the other end is bolted to ducting fixed to the structure. A rigid pipe joining them cracks — from thermal expansion, from vibration, or from being fractionally misaligned at installation. The bellows absorbs all three.

8. The tariff mechanism — why one quarter halved

This is the single most instructive episode in the company's short listed life, and it is a pure industry-structure story rather than an operational failure. Nothing broke inside the factory.

Under normal conditions, a Tier-1 customer holds tooling inventory in a US warehouse and restocks in batches. When US tariffs on Indian goods went to 50%, holding that inventory became expensive, so customers switched to drop-shipment only — ordering just what a job needed that week and deferring everything non-urgent. Unimech's order intake did not fall because aircraft stopped being built; it fell because a link in the chain changed its inventory policy.

The general lesson, beyond Unimech

A supplier this far down the chain does not see end demand — it sees its customer's ordering policy. Those two can diverge sharply and for several quarters at a time. Any component or tooling business with a high fixed-cost base and episodic orders will show this shape: long stretches of stable revenue punctuated by a quarter where the operating margin nearly vanishes, without anything in the underlying market having changed.

11. What the company does not disclose

Stated as gaps rather than filled with inference, because the difference between "not reported" and "I could not find it" matters. Each of these is answerable and none has been answered on any of the eight calls:

  • The conversion rate of qualifications into serial orders. An analyst asked this directly in Q1FY27 — what proportion of SKUs qualified over the last two to three years actually became recurring business. The FAI count is the company's headline growth indicator, and without a conversion rate it cannot be judged.
  • Customer concentration in the tooling business. Hobel's is disclosed (two OEM groups ≈93%). Unimech's own is not, in a segment where the buyers are a small number of Tier-1 licensees.
  • The split of revenue between new-build tooling and MRO tooling. These have different cyclicality — MRO is the annuity, new-build follows programme rates — and they are reported as one number.
  • Repeat versus first-time revenue. The whole moat argument rests on requalification cost deterring switching; the share of revenue that is genuinely repeat business would evidence it.
  • Segment-level order book. Only the Unimech/Hobel split is given, so it is not possible to see whether the nuclear or precision pipeline is actually building.

12. Glossary

OEM
Original Equipment Manufacturer — the company whose name is on the product. Airbus and Boeing for aircraft; GE, Safran, Pratt & Whitney and Rolls-Royce for engines.
Tier-1 / Tier-2
Position in the supply chain. A Tier-1 sells directly to the OEM; a Tier-2 sells to a Tier-1. Unimech is mostly Tier-2.
Tool licensee
A company licensed by an engine OEM to manufacture and sell that engine's tooling. Unimech's main tooling customer type.
MRO
Maintenance, Repair and Overhaul — the shops that strip, inspect and rebuild engines through their service life.
GSE
Ground Support Equipment — carts, dollies, stands and handling rigs used around an aircraft or engine on the ground.
FAI
First Article Inspection — making one unit and submitting a full evidence dossier so the customer can approve that part number for supply.
SKU
A distinct part number. "6,300+ qualified SKUs" means that many individual items Unimech is approved to supply.
AS9100D
The aerospace quality-management standard. Without it you cannot be an aerospace supplier at all.
NADCAP
Accreditation for special processes — heat treatment, coating, welding, non-destructive testing — audited separately from the quality system.
Build-to-print
You manufacture to the customer's drawing. Lower engineering content.
Build-to-spec
You design the item to meet the customer's functional requirement. Higher engineering content, stickier.
LTA
Long-Term Agreement — a multi-year supply contract, as opposed to a one-off purchase order.
PO-to-PO
Business that arrives one purchase order at a time, with no contractual commitment to the next one.
Drop shipment
Shipping directly to the point of use instead of into a customer's warehouse — what customers switched to under the 50% tariff.
FTWZ
Free Trade Warehousing Zone — a bonded warehouse on Indian soil where a customer can hold stock before it is formally imported or exported.
BHP
Brake horsepower — the engine-size range Hobel's bellows serve, 900 to 9,000 BHP.
