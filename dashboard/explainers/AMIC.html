How the forging business actually works

An industry primer for Amic Forging — why a part gets forged, the machine that defines this kind of shop, and where the money is actually made

This page explains the industry, not the investment case. What follows is the mechanism: why a load-bearing component gets forged rather than cut or cast, what an open-die shop can do that a press line cannot, how Amic's own line runs from billet to certified part, how a supplier earns the right to quote at all, and why this company's revenue can fall while its profit triples.

1. Why forge at all — the grain-flow argument

Steel is not uniform inside. Rolling and solidification leave it with a directional structure — a "grain" — and a part fails along that structure long before it fails across it. There are three ways to get a shape, and they treat the grain completely differently.

Forging deforms hot metal so the grain flows around the finished shape. Machining from a solid bar cuts straight through it, leaving exposed grain ends where stress concentrates. Casting has no directional grain at all, plus porosity risk. That single difference is why a refinery, a wind turbine or a nuclear pressure boundary specifies a forged part and will not accept a cheaper substitute.

2. Open die — the machine that defines this kind of shop

"Forging" covers two quite different machines, and which one a company owns determines almost everything else about it — what it can make, who its customers are, and how it earns a margin.

Closed-die forging puts the hot billet between two shaped dies that enclose it and squeeze. You get a repeatable near-net shape at speed — but you need a die for every part, so it only pays when thousands or millions of identical pieces are wanted. Open-die forging hammers the billet between simple tools while a manipulator turns and moves it. Nothing encloses the workpiece. It is slower and skill-intensive, but it needs no part-specific die at all — so it can economically make one of something, and it can make something very large.

Amic runs open die: a 6 metric-tonne electro-hydraulic hammer and a 1 MT pneumatic hammer, each worked with a manipulator. Almost everything else on this page follows from that one choice.

4. What Amic makes, and who buys it

The RHP lists ten products with photographs, and names the industries they serve: heavy engineering, steel, oil & gas, petrochemicals, chemicals, refineries, thermal power, nuclear power, hydro power, cement and sugar. Read that list once and the demand cycle becomes clear — this is capital expenditure by process industries, not consumer or vehicle demand.

5. Pre-qualification — how you earn the right to quote

You cannot simply offer to forge a nuclear trunnion. Before a customer will accept a quote, the supplier has to be pre-qualified — a process the company describes in its own risk factors, and which is the closest thing this business has to a moat.

"Majority of our customers require forging manufacturers to undergo pre-qualification processes. These processes evaluate both the technical ability to provide relevant products with the exact specifications needed by the end-user, and the production capabilities of the supplier. These processes generally take time to complete and involves incurring significant upfront expenses in learning and meeting customer qualification requirements."
— Amic Forging RHP, Risk Factor 3

6. Job-shop economics — revenue fell, profit tripled

FY25 is the single most instructive year in Amic's short public record, and it makes a point that applies to the whole open-die trade: in a bespoke forging business, what you make matters far more than how much of it you make.

7. The value ladder — from billet to certified part

A forging leaves the hammer worth a fraction of what the customer eventually pays. Everything after it — heat treatment, machining, testing, documentation — adds value to the same piece of metal, and how far up that ladder a shop can go is most of what determines its margin.

8. Backward integration — the plan to make its own steel

Amic buys its raw material — alloy steel, low-carbon and medium-carbon steel — on the spot market, with no long-term supply agreements. For a business whose input is the dominant cost and whose output is quoted job by job, that is a real exposure, and the company says so in its risk factors. Its stated answer is to go upstream.

9. Capacity and the utilisation record

Amic's RHP prints three years of installed capacity against actual production — an unusually clean disclosure, and the one place where growth is checkable against physical output rather than revenue.

10. The competitive structure, in the company's own words

Amic describes its market plainly in the RHP, and the description is worth reading closely because it says who actually wins work in this trade.

"The market for forging is highly competitive with few organized players and localised smaller unorganised players. Important factors affecting competition in the sector include, existing relationship with the clients, reliability, quality, price, past track record."
— Amic Forging RHP, Risk Factor 9

12. What is not disclosed

  • Customer names and concentration. The company flags that its business "largely depends upon our top ten customers" — but never quantifies what share those ten represent, and names none of them. For a job shop where a handful of capital projects can dominate a year, that is the largest single gap in the file.
  • Product mix. Ten products are pictured; none is sized. There is no split between wind, nuclear, oil & gas and general engineering, so the end-market exposure in §4 is inferred from what the components are, not reported.
  • Order book. Nothing equivalent is published, so forward visibility rests on utilisation and revenue alone — and the company separately warns that orders "may be delayed, modified, cancelled or not fully paid for".
  • What drove FY25's margin. The tripling of profit on lower revenue is attributed to "cost control and value-enhancing initiatives" with no decomposition between price, mix, machining share and input cost.
  • Capacity after the June-2026 expansion. The RHP table ends at FY23; the new installed tonnage has not been restated in the same format.
  • Timing or cost of the steel-melting plan. Stated as intent in the RHP and reaffirmed in FY25, with no capex figure, location or date.
  • No earnings calls at all. As a BSE-SME reporting half-yearly, there is no management commentary, no guidance and no analyst questioning anywhere in the record.

13. Glossary

Billet
A cut length of steel bar — the starting stock for a forging.
Open-die forging
Hot metal hammered between simple tools that do not enclose it, with a manipulator turning the piece between blows. No part-specific die; suits one-off and large components. Amic's process.
Closed-die forging
Hot metal squeezed between two shaped dies that enclose it, giving a repeatable near-net shape. Needs a die per part, so it pays only at volume.
Ring rolling
A third technique: a pierced doughnut of metal rolled out into a seamless ring.
Grain flow
The directional structure inside worked steel. Forging makes it follow the part's shape; machining from bar cuts across it.
Manipulator
The machine that grips, lifts and rotates a hot workpiece under the hammer — what makes open-die work possible at size.
Annealing / normalising
Controlled heating and cooling after forging to relieve stress and refine grain structure.
Quenching
Rapid cooling in water or oil to set hardness. Amic's heat-treatment plant has both.
Bogie hearth furnace
A heat-treatment furnace with a wheeled hearth that rolls large parts in and out.
Ultrasonic testing
Non-destructive inspection for internal flaws, done on incoming metal and on the finished part.
Pre-qualification
The customer's evaluation of a supplier's technical ability and production capability before it may quote. Slow, costly, and losable.
Ingot casting
Pouring molten steel into moulds to make the stock a forge then works. The upstream step Amic says it intends to add.
Near-net shape
A forging close enough to final dimensions that little machining is needed afterwards.
Tube sheet / channel cover
Thick forged plates in a heat exchanger — the tube sheet carries the tube ends, the channel cover closes the housing.
Trunnion
A load-bearing pivot mounting. Amic lists one made for a nuclear power plant.
