Three clocks and a map

An industry primer for Kolte-Patil Developers: what a residential developer actually sells, why the same apartment appears in three different numbers years apart, how land is obtained without buying it — and why the price per square foot, the EBITDA margin and "net cash" in its results each mislead
Built 11-Nov-2026 · Sourced from Kolte-Patil's Q2FY27 results (3-Nov-2026), its Q2FY26 → Q2FY27 investor presentations and press releases, the last three earnings-call transcripts that exist (Feb, May and Jul-2025), and NSE filings · Companion to Company Profiles/KOLTE.md

This page explains the business, not the investment case. It carries no valuation, no conviction and no target; those live in the profile. What follows is the mechanism: what a home-builder physically sells, when each part of a sale reaches each line of the accounts, where the land comes from, and how to read the three numbers in Kolte-Patil's recent results that are easiest to misread.

1. The product, physically: a phase of a project, sold by the square foot

A residential developer does not sell buildings. It sells promises of homes in buildings that mostly do not exist yet. The unit of business is the project — a parcel of land with permission to build a certain floor area on it — and a large project is released to the market in phases, each registered separately under RERA (the Real Estate Regulation Act) and sold separately.

Kolte-Patil is a Pune developer, founded 1991, with a smaller business in Mumbai and a token one in Bengaluru. It sells under two brands — Kolte-Patil (mid-premium and premium) and 24K (premium luxury) — and its single largest asset is Life Republic, a 400-plus-acre township near Pune's Hinjewadi IT hub with more than 15 Mn sq ft already sold and about 16 Mn sq ft still to develop.

Two details on Figure 1 matter for everything later. First, the volume unit is the saleable square foot, a measure that includes a share of common areas; its relationship to the usable "carpet" area the buyer actually lives in is not disclosed, so ₹ per square foot cannot be compared to a competitor that reports on carpet area. Second, one launch can dominate a quarter: Vyana was 60% of Q2FY27's pre-sales.

2. The mechanism: one home, three clocks

This is the one fact that makes the rest of Kolte-Patil's numbers intelligible. Every home it sells passes through three different lines of its reports at three different times, and the times can be years apart:

  • Pre-sales (also "sales value" or "bookings") counts the home's full price on the day the buyer signs. It is an operating number the company reports; it is not in the audited accounts.
  • Collections is the cash the buyer actually pays — a booking amount, then instalments linked to construction progress, then the balance at possession.
  • Revenue and profit are recognised only when the home is complete and handed over. The company calls this the completed-contract method: "revenue recognition is based on completion and possession" (30-Jul-2025 call).

The consequence is that the profit-and-loss statement is the slowest and least informative of the three. A year of record sales can arrive with a loss, because the homes sold are years from completion; a quarter in which several towers finish can show more revenue than the whole previous year. The company's own results notes say it plainly: "results of a quarter may not be representative of profits/(losses) for the year."

Why the buyer's money matters more than the P&L

Because buyers pay as the building rises, a developer that sells well is largely funded by its customers. That money sits on the balance sheet as a liability — an advance for a home not yet delivered — until the home is handed over. At 30-Sep-2026 Kolte-Patil's "other current liabilities", where customer advances are carried, stood at ₹4,519 Cr against ₹1,359 Cr of shareholders' equity (the filing does not split the line). MY INFERENCE For a business like this the cash and the advances say more about health than the profit line does.

3. What the three clocks look like in Kolte-Patil's own numbers

Figure 3 puts the three series for the last six quarters on one axis. Pre-sales and collections move like a business; revenue moves like a calendar of building completions.

Read across a year rather than a quarter and the gap is stark. In FY26 the company booked ₹2,605 Cr of homes and collected ₹2,689 Cr of cash, but recognised only ₹735 Cr of revenue and reported a ₹38 Cr loss. In the next quarter alone it recognised ₹920 Cr, because about 1.27 Mn sq ft of homes reached completion. Nothing about the business changed between those two periods; the completion calendar did.

4. Three places, three economics: Life Republic, Pune and Mumbai

The company's land bank — the homes it can build over the next decade — sits in three very different markets. Life Republic is a township on land the company has held for years; the rest of Pune is a mix of owned and joint-development land; Mumbai is almost entirely society redevelopment, where an old building is torn down and replaced (see §6).

The three places differ in price per square foot by a factor of three to four, and they differ in how much of their land is ready to sell. Life Republic's 15.9 Mn sq ft is three-quarters land bank; Pune outside it has 4.2 Mn sq ft waiting on approvals; Mumbai is small in area and large in value. Which of these sells in a given quarter changes almost every reported ratio — the subject of §8.

5. Three ways to get land

A developer's most important decision is how it gets land, because that sets how much money goes in before the first home is sold, and therefore both the margin and the return. Kolte-Patil uses all three of the models common in Indian residential development; its 2026 decks list "outright, redevelopment, JDA & JV."

The arithmetic behind the trade-off is simple. MY INFERENCE In an outright purchase the developer pays for land years before it sells anything, so it needs a high margin to earn a return on that money. In a joint development or a redevelopment the landowner is paid out of the sales themselves — a share of revenue, or new flats — so the margin per square foot is lower but far less money is tied up. A mix shift toward joint developments and Mumbai redevelopment therefore lowers the reported margin even if every project is doing what it was meant to. Kolte-Patil's then Group CEO said exactly this in February 2025: "In redevelopment, joint developments it's not just margin, it is more of an IRR-based business model."

6. Mumbai society redevelopment, step by step

Mumbai is full of residential buildings put up in the 1960s–80s by housing societies — co-operatives that own the land and the building. As the buildings age, and as city rules allow more floor area on the same plot, a society can hand its plot to a developer: the developer demolishes the old building, rebuilds it taller, gives the existing members new and usually larger flats at no cost, and sells the additional flats on the open market. The developer never buys the land, which is why the company calls this model one of "lower capital intensity."

The model's strength is also its slowness. Nothing can be built until every family in the old building has moved out — the vacation step — and that requires the society's consent, the approvals and the developer's commitment to pay the members' rent while they are away. In August 2026 Kolte-Patil signed six such projects, ~₹6,000 Cr of GDV across ~2 Mn sq ft in Santacruz, Andheri, Oshiwara, Versova, Ghatkopar and Vashi, guided for launch "over the next 6 to 12 months, subject to requisite approvals." At ~₹30,000 of GDV per saleable square foot (my division of ~₹6,000 Cr by ~2 Mn sq ft), they are valued at roughly four times Life Republic's ~₹6,900 — which is why a small amount of Mumbai area can move the company-wide averages in §8.

7. 🔴 Reading the numbers (I): the P&L follows completions, not sales

Q2FY27 is a clean demonstration of §2. It was the best selling quarter in the company's history — ₹1,019 Cr of pre-sales, 1.30 Mn sq ft, both records — and one of its thinnest for profit.

The press release's headline, "Healthy EBITDA Margins in H1 FY27: 17%", is correct and needs three qualifiers, all from the company's own filing. It is a half-year figure, so it includes Q1. It is measured on total income, so it includes other income. And it is an average of a 22.0% first quarter and a 4.9% second. On the narrower definition the company uses in its Reg 52(4) ratio table — operating profit excluding other income, over revenue — Q2's margin was −0.09%: ₹334.4 Cr of revenue against ₹334.7 Cr of operating cost. Q2's ₹9 Cr of profit before tax is essentially its ₹17 Cr of other income, less interest and depreciation.

What this is not

It is not deterioration. Completed area fell from ~1.27 Mn sq ft in Q1 to ~0.36 Mn sq ft in Q2, while staff and overhead costs — which a developer expenses every quarter — kept running. The company said as much in July 2025: "margins look impaired on account of lower revenue recognition coupled with fixed cost recognition which is charged to PnL." The way to read any single quarter's margin here is alongside that quarter's completed area, which the company discloses only in its press releases and only approximately.

8. 🔴 Reading the numbers (II): average realisation is a map

"Average realisation" — pre-sales value divided by area sold — looks like a price. On this company it is mostly a measure of where the quarter's sales happened, because a square foot in Mumbai sells for about ₹19,000 and a square foot in Pune for ₹7,300–8,300 (Q1 and Q2FY27 regional tables).

Two headline readings both mislead in the same way. In Q1FY27 the company reported realisation up 29% with sales value flat, which reads as "price is doing the work while volume falls 22%". Split by region, Pune's own price rose about 9%; the rest was Mumbai going from 3% of sales to 30%, and Pune's area sold actually fell about a third, cushioned by Mumbai. In Q2FY27 the company reported realisation flat with volume up 52%, which reads as "growth is all volume". Pune's own price rose about 2% year on year, Mumbai fell back to 5% of sales, and 60% of the quarter's value came from a single launch. MY INFERENCE on the reading; the arithmetic uses only deck figures.

The same effect, one level down

Inside Pune the mix matters too. Life Republic sold ₹171 Cr in Q2FY27 against ₹365 Cr a year earlier (−53%), and ₹212 Cr against ~₹354 Cr in Q1 (−40%). The township was 51% of FY26 pre-sales and 17% of Q2FY27's. The record quarter therefore combined a new project's launch with the established township selling at half its year-earlier pace — two movements a single total cannot show. The company has not explained the Life Republic decline, and there has been no call at which to ask.

9. 🔴 Reading the numbers (III): what counts as debt

The Q2FY27 deck reports "Net Debt ₹(466) Cr" — a net cash position. The figure is arithmetically right on the company's definition, which subtracts zero-coupon NCDs (non-convertible debentures that pay no interest along the way) from debt before netting cash.

The zero-coupon NCDs are real borrowings: they are inside the ₹909 Cr on the balance sheet, rated by CARE and CRISIL, and secured on project land and receivables. What makes them different is how they are repaid — out of collections from specific "earmarked" units as those units are sold, together with a premium that is also linked to those collections. MY INFERENCE That makes them closer to project-level financing that shares in sales than to a bank loan, which is presumably why the company nets them out; it is also why "zero coupon" does not mean "zero cost" — on the oldest listed series the premium paid so far equals 51% of the principal redeemed.

A related effect hides the cost of borrowing from the P&L. Interest is capitalised — added to the cost of the homes under construction — and reaches the income statement only when those homes complete, inside cost of sales. In H1FY27 the company paid ₹60.6 Cr of interest in cash and showed ₹5.6 Cr as finance cost. So the P&L's interest line understates the cost of debt in every quarter, and the gross margin at completion absorbs it.

10. The counterweight: approvals, concentration and the cash that leaves

Customer money funds the build — but only half of it reaches the site

Of every ₹100 a buyer paid in FY26, about ₹50 went into construction and another ₹21 into overheads and taxes; land, approval premia, TDR (transferable development rights) and payouts to joint-venture partners took a further ₹19. About ₹10 was left before any borrowing or repayment. Customer funding is real, but it is not free cash: most of it is committed to building the homes it paid for.

The approvals clock, and an unexplained change in Mumbai

Land banked is not land sellable. Pune outside Life Republic has 4.2 Mn sq ft "under approval"; the six new Mumbai projects are guided "subject to requisite approvals". And between the Q1 and Q2FY27 decks, Mumbai's portfolio fell from ₹8,900 Cr of GDV and 3.49 Mn sq ft to ₹7,400 Cr and 2.89 Mn sq ft, in a quarter in which Mumbai sold ₹48 Cr and 0.03 Mn sq ft. The boilerplate count of Mumbai projects moved from nineteen (nine future) to seventeen (seven future). The deck does not say which projects left or why. The deck also changed its project-counting method this quarter, so counts may be re-based — GDV is not a count.

Concentration and inputs

Life Republic was more than half of FY26's sales and is the largest block of land; Pune as a whole was 95% of Q2FY27's. Construction cost — roughly half of every rupee collected — is steel, cement, labour and contractors, none of which the company breaks out; no commodity series tracked for this watchlist maps onto it with any precision.

12. What is not disclosed

  • Any management Q&A since 30-Jul-2025. The calls for Q2FY26, Q3FY26, Q4FY26, Q1FY27 and Q2FY27 were each declined. Every mechanism quote on this page predates them.
  • The loading between saleable and carpet area, so ₹ per sq ft cannot be compared with developers that report on carpet area.
  • Completed area by quarter is given only approximately ("~1.27", "~1.63 Mn sq ft") in press releases, and not by project.
  • Project-level or model-level margins beyond the 2025 call ranges.
  • Society redevelopment terms — members' area entitlement, rent, corpus — for any Mumbai project.
  • What the ₹1,178 Cr of trade payables are: the line rose ₹518 Cr in six months, almost exactly the ₹524 Cr inventory build, with no split between contractors, landowners and approval dues.
  • How much of the ₹336 Cr of "other balances with banks" is restricted escrow, and the holders and terms of any zero-coupon NCDs beyond the four listed series (the deck's ₹432 Cr total is ₹81 Cr more than those four's outstanding principal).
  • Why Mumbai's portfolio fell ₹1,500 Cr of GDV in Q2FY27, and why Life Republic's pre-sales halved year on year.
  • The ~₹150 Cr GST Section 74 matter disclosed in June 2026 is not mentioned in the Q2FY27 results, release or deck.

13. Glossary · Sources

Pre-sales / sales value
The full price of homes for which sale agreements were signed in the period. An operating figure, not revenue.
Collections
Cash actually received from buyers in the period.
CCM
Completed-contract method: revenue and profit on a home are recognised only when it is complete and possession is given.
Saleable area
The area a home is priced on, including a share of walls, balconies and common areas. Also "super built-up".
Carpet area
The usable floor area inside a home's walls; the figure RERA requires sale agreements to state.
Realisation
Pre-sales value divided by saleable area sold — a blended ₹ per sq ft.
GDV
Gross development value: the company's estimate of total sale value if a project is fully built and sold at current prices. Not revenue, and not profit.
RERA
Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016. Requires project registration and that most buyer money for a project be kept in a separate bank account for that project.
OC
Occupancy certificate — the municipal permission to occupy a finished building; in practice the trigger for possession.
JDA
Joint development agreement: the landowner contributes land and receives a share of revenue or of the built area.
Society redevelopment
Rebuilding an old co-operative housing society's building: members get new flats, the developer sells the extra floor area.
FSI / TDR
Floor space index — the floor area allowed on a plot. Transferable development rights — extra floor area bought from elsewhere and loaded onto a plot.
Zero-coupon NCD
A debenture paying no periodic interest; Kolte-Patil's are redeemed with a premium linked to sales of earmarked units.
Capitalised interest
Interest added to the cost of inventory under construction instead of being expensed; it reaches the P&L through cost of sales at completion.
Reg 52(4)
The SEBI rule requiring companies with listed debt to publish a set of ratios with their results, including an operating margin that excludes other income.
