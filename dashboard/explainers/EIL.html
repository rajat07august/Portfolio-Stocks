How refinery engineering actually works

An industry primer for Engineers India — what a design consultancy sells, why the same rupee of revenue is worth three times more in one segment than the other, and why the order book matters more than the order book
Built 31-Aug-2026 · Sourced from EIL's 7 official transcripts and the Q1FY27 results and filings on disk · Companion to Company Profiles/EIL.md

This page explains the industry, not the investment case. What follows is the mechanism: what a refinery owner actually buys when it hires an engineering consultancy, the two very different businesses EIL runs under one roof, why a quarter with falling revenue can be a much better quarter, what an asset-light design house looks like on a balance sheet, and why the thing to watch is not the backlog but the rate at which it is being refilled.

1. What a refinery owner is actually buying

A refinery expansion does not begin with steel. It begins with a question — can this be done, at what cost, and to what design? — and years of engineering answer it before a single vessel is ordered. That engineering is a purchased service, and it is EIL's business.

The work runs in stages, and each stage is a separate contract with its own price. The early stages are cheap and enormously influential; the later ones are expensive and largely mechanical.

2. Two businesses under one roof

EIL reports two segments, and they are not two flavours of the same thing. They differ in what is sold, what is risked, how much capital is consumed and — above all — what a rupee of revenue is worth.

3. Why mix is the entire P&L

Q1FY27 is the cleanest demonstration of this you will find in any Indian filing. Turnover fell 6.5%. Profit before tax rose 55%. Nothing about the market changed — the mix did.

4. ₹100 of revenue, in each segment

Put the two segments on the same ₹100 note and the strategy explains itself.

5. What an asset-light design house looks like

Almost every other industrial business on this watchlist converts capital into product. A design consultancy converts people into product — and the balance sheet shows it.

6. The order engine — and why intake beats backlog

A backlog is a stock; intake is a flow. For a business that bills against a book it has already won, the backlog tells you about the next two years and the intake tells you about everything after that. Right now those two numbers are saying different things.

7. Who buys, and the fact that the owner is also the customer

EIL's client list is short, concentrated, and mostly owned by the same shareholder that owns EIL.

8. The capex cycle, and why a consultancy feels it first

Everything EIL sells sits at the front of somebody else's capital expenditure. That position has a consequence most investors get backwards: a design house is an early indicator of the industrial cycle, not a lagging one.

9. The new frontiers — all of them consultancy-first

Three markets have opened that were not in this business five years ago, and the useful pattern is that EIL enters each of them at the design end — the high-margin, low-capital, early-cycle end described in §1.

10. Where the profit actually comes from

One habit protects you in a business like this: separate the operating result from everything else attached to it. In Q1FY27 the headline and the operating story were genuinely different numbers.

12. What is not disclosed

  • Order inflow, reconciled. Two figures were given on the same call — ₹514cr for Q1 and ₹2,750cr "as of today" — and no bridge between them was offered or requested.
  • Headcount and utilisation. For a business whose capacity is engineers and whose product is billable hours, neither the number of engineers nor their utilisation rate is published — the single most useful operating metric for a consultancy is absent.
  • Client concentration. The major PSU clients are named qualitatively; no top-client revenue share is given, in a business where a handful of owners award most of the work.
  • Order book ageing. ₹14,424cr is disclosed by segment but not by expected execution period, so the rate at which it converts to revenue has to be inferred.
  • Overseas revenue and margin split. The Middle East and Africa are discussed in commentary; neither revenue nor profitability is broken out.
  • The pricing basis of consultancy contracts — man-hour rate, lump sum or percentage of project cost — is not described, so the improvement from 17% to 24% cannot be attributed between rate, utilisation and mix.

13. Glossary

Consultancy & Engineering
EIL's core segment: feasibility studies, process design, FEED, detailed engineering and project management. Sells hours; carries no money risk.
LSTK / Turnkey
Lump-Sum Turnkey. EIL takes the whole project at a fixed price, buying equipment and hiring construction. Large tickets, ~7.5% margin, real cost risk.
FEED
Front-End Engineering Design — the stage that converts a concept into a costed, specified design. It fixes the budget the owner will be held to.
PMC
Project Management Consultancy — running the tender, supervising the contractor and protecting the owner's interest during construction.
Detailed engineering
Every drawing, specification and datasheet needed to actually build the plant.
Process licensing
Rights to a specific chemical process route. Choosing it early determines what the plant can make and at what cost.
Order book / backlog
Work won but not yet executed. A stock, billed out over three to five years in consultancy.
Order inflow / intake
New work won in a period. The flow that refills the backlog — the leading indicator.
Book-to-bill
Intake divided by revenue. Below one means the backlog is shrinking.
MMTPA
Million metric tonnes per annum — how refining capacity is measured.
SMR
Small Modular Reactor — compact nuclear units. EIL has secured SMR work from NPCIL.
SNG
Synthetic Natural Gas — the output of the coal gasification project EIL is executing for NTPC.
RFCL
Ramagundam Fertilizers & Chemicals — the joint venture whose profit now flows into EIL's consolidated result.
