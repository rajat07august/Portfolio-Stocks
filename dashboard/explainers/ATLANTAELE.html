1. What a transformer does, and why the grid needs so many

Electricity leaves a power station at a voltage far too low to travel any distance. Push it down a line at that voltage and the current is high, and high current turns energy into heat in the wire. The fix is to raise the voltage, which lowers the current for the same power, and then lower it again at the other end. A transformer is the device that does both — and the grid needs one at every junction where the voltage changes.

That is why a country rebuilding its grid buys an enormous number of them, and why they are sold to utilities rather than to consumers.

2. The voltage ladder — the whole industry in one axis

If you understand one thing about this industry, make it this. A transformer maker's economics are set by the voltage class it is approved to build — not by how many transformers it makes, and not by how large its factory is. Every rung up the ladder means bigger units, fewer competitors, longer approval cycles and larger orders.

3. What is actually inside the steel box

A transformer has no moving parts. It is a stack of specially grained steel with copper wound around it, sitting in a tank of oil — and almost all of its cost is in those three materials.

4. The approval barrier — why you cannot simply build a bigger one

A utility will not buy a 220 kV transformer from a manufacturer whose plant it has not audited and whose prototype has not been destroyed in a test. Approval is plant-specific and voltage-class-specific — which means a company that is approved at 220 kV in one factory is not thereby approved at 400 kV, nor at 220 kV in a different factory.

5. Why capacity is counted in MVA

A transformer maker does not report how many transformers it made, because a 5 MVA unit and a 500 MVA unit are not comparable objects. Capacity and output are measured in MVA — mega volt-amperes, the electrical throughput the machine can handle. It is the industry's tonne.

6. Why renewables need more transformers than coal did

A coal plant is large, close to the grid backbone and runs at a steady output. A solar or wind farm is none of those things — and each difference adds transformers.

7. Revenue per MVA is a commodity price

The temptation with an MVA-denominated business is to divide revenue by MVA and call it realisation. Management warns against exactly that, and the warning is worth taking seriously because it is the same trap as every other pass-through business on this watchlist.

"Revenue per MVA is directly related to the commodity pricing… Per MVA pricing will naturally not be a factor to highlight how the industry is performing."
— Chairman & Managing Director, FY26 call

8. The order book and its execution window

In a build-to-order business, the order book is the forward revenue — but only the part that can actually be executed inside the year tells you anything about the next four quarters.

10. The inputs, and where they come from

Three materials dominate the bill: CRGO electrical steel, copper and insulating oil. Two of the three have had a supply event in the past year, and one is subject to a live trade investigation.

12. What is not disclosed

  • Margin by voltage class. The entire thesis is a move from 220 kV to 400 kV and 765 kV, and management explicitly declines to estimate what those will earn: "it is not right to comment as to what kind of margins would we be earning… since we are going to manufacture these products for the first time." Honest, and it means the economics of the growth story are unknown.
  • Order book split by voltage class in rupees. A percentage is given for 220 kV; the 400 kV portion is described as deliberately restricted but not sized.
  • Capacity utilisation by plant. Q1FY27 MVA was broken out for Vadod and Ankhi when an analyst asked, but plant-level utilisation is not a standing disclosure — and nameplate assumes a product mix that is not stated.
  • Realisation per MVA by class — which, given §7, is arguably the right thing not to publish, but it means mix effects cannot be separated from commodity effects by an outside reader.
  • The CRGO sourcing split — how much comes from which origin, which is what would let a reader size the anti-dumping exposure.
  • Order-to-delivery lead times, which in a stretched-supply industry is the single best indicator of whether the shortage is easing.

13. Glossary

Transformer
A device with no moving parts that raises or lowers voltage using two coils wound on a shared steel core. The grid needs one wherever the voltage changes.
Power transformer
The large, high-voltage units used in transmission and at substations. Atlanta's business — 79% of Q1FY27 revenue.
Distribution transformer
The small pole- or pad-mounted units at the consumer end. A different market, which Atlanta states it is not in.
kV
Kilovolt — the voltage class. The rung on the ladder, and the thing a manufacturer is approved against.
MVA
Mega volt-ampere — the throughput rating. The industry's unit for both capacity and output, because units of vastly different size are not comparable.
CRGO
Cold-Rolled Grain-Oriented electrical steel. The core material, largely imported, and the input under a live anti-dumping investigation.
Windings
The copper coils around the core. The ratio of turns between the two windings sets the voltage change.
Bushing
The insulated fitting where a conductor passes through the tank wall.
Radiator
The finned panels on the tank that shed heat from the circulating oil.
Ester oil
A synthetic or natural-ester insulating fluid used as an alternative to mineral oil — what Atlanta had contracted when mineral oil ran short.
Short-circuit test
A destructive type test, in India performed at CPRI, that a prototype must survive before a design and plant are approved for a class.
PGCIL approval
Power Grid Corporation's qualification of a specific plant for a specific voltage class. Plant-specific and class-specific — not transferable.
EHV
Extra High Voltage — broadly 400 kV and above. The capability Atlanta acquired with Atlanta Trafo.
Evacuation
Transmission capacity built to carry generation — typically renewable — from where it is produced to where it is used.
