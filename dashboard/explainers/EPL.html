1. The product — what a laminated tube actually is

Pick up a tube of toothpaste, face cream or hair colour. It feels like a single piece of soft plastic. It is not. It is a flat multi-layer sheet — a laminate — that has been printed, rolled into a cylinder, welded along one edge, and had a moulded shoulder and cap attached at the top. The bottom is left open, because that is where the brand fills it before crimping it shut.

That construction is the whole business. The layers do different jobs: the outer layer takes the printing, an inner layer touches the product and must not react with it, and somewhere in the middle sits a barrier — historically aluminium foil, increasingly a polymer — whose job is to stop oxygen, moisture and aroma passing through in either direction. A fluoride toothpaste that loses its active ingredient through the pack wall is a failed product, and the barrier is what prevents it.

2. Three ways to make a tube, and why the choice matters

"Tube" is not one technology. Three compete, and a brand's choice is driven by barrier need, decoration quality, order size and cost.

3. How a tube gets made — and what EPL does not do

The process runs from a roll of film to a palletised box of empty tubes. Everything upstream of "filling" is EPL's; everything from filling onwards belongs to the brand.

4. Why the plant sits next to the customer

EPL runs 20+ plants across 11 countries. That looks inefficient for a company making one product — until you consider what a truckload of empty tubes actually contains.

5. The pass-through mechanic — why one quarter grew 25% and lost margin

This is the single most useful thing to understand about EPL's reported numbers, and it is pure industry structure rather than performance.

Every customer contract is priced on a landed-cost basis. When polymer prices rise, EPL raises the price it charges, and recovers not just resin but freight and currency movement too. CFO and CEO were explicit on the Q1FY27 call: "all our customer contracts are on a landed cost basis. So currency depreciation also gets recovered. Freight also saw sharp increase and we have been able to recover that from the customers."

That protects the business — and it distorts the percentages, in a specific and predictable direction.

8. Recyclability as a moat, not a cost

The barrier layer that makes a tube work is also what makes it hard to recycle: a laminate of several different materials, including aluminium foil, cannot go into a single recycling stream. The industry's answer is a mono-material structure — a tube built almost entirely from one polymer family, with a polymer barrier instead of foil, so the whole pack can be recycled together.

Replacing that layer is not a marketing decision. It changes how the pack performs, so the brand has to re-test it — and that re-testing is where the competitive advantage is created.

9. Five regions, one product

Because plants follow customers, EPL reports as five regions rather than as one global business. In Q1FY27 all five grew double digits at once — which is unusual, and worth seeing in proportion.

12. What EPL does not disclose

Stated as gaps rather than filled with inference:

  • Tube volumes. Growth is reported in revenue, which is exactly the number that pass-through pricing distorts. A unit count would settle in one line what §5 needs three paragraphs to explain.
  • Realisation per tube, by category. Management says B&C carries a higher selling price and the same percentage margin — so absolute profit per tube is the real mix argument, and it is never quantified.
  • Region-level margin. Europe is discussed qualitatively as below its mid-teens target; the other four regions' margins are not broken out at all.
  • Capacity and utilisation. For a business whose thesis is "capex leads revenue", there is no disclosed capacity number or utilisation rate to judge how much revenue the installed base can carry.
  • Customer concentration. Oral care is stated to be dominated by a few customers; the actual top-customer share is not given.
  • The split between laminated and extruded tubes, even as extruded capacity is named as a growth enabler.

13. Glossary

Laminated tube
A tube made from a printed multi-layer sheet rolled into a cylinder and welded along a side seam. Also called ABL (aluminium barrier laminate) or PBL (plastic barrier laminate) depending on the barrier used.
Extruded tube
A tube whose body is extruded as a seamless plastic cylinder rather than made from a sheet. No side seam; different barrier options.
Barrier layer
The layer inside the wall that stops oxygen, moisture and aroma passing through. Aluminium foil performs best; polymer barriers allow recyclability.
Mono-material
A pack built from a single polymer family so it can be recycled as one stream. The industry's route to a recyclable tube.
Shoulder
The moulded cone at the top of the tube carrying the neck and orifice, formed onto the sleeve.
Tail seal
The crimped, heat-sealed bottom edge — closed by the brand after filling, not by the tube maker.
Filling line
The customer's machine that loads empty tubes, injects product and seals the tail. Where a tube proves itself.
Landed cost
A contract basis in which the price charged recovers raw material, freight and currency movement. EPL states all customer contracts are on this basis.
Pass-through
The revenue effect of that recovery: sales rise with input costs without any extra tube being sold, inflating growth and diluting percentage margin.
Underlying growth
Growth with the pass-through effect stripped out — the number to compare across quarters.
B&C
Beauty & Cosmetics. Reported by EPL inside "personal care & beyond", which also includes pharma, food, home and hygiene.
Neoseam
EPL's technology for a laminated tube with no visible seam line, allowing 360° printing.
Platina
EPL's brand for its sustainable, recyclable tube range.
EcoVadis / CDP
Third-party sustainability rating schemes. EPL holds EcoVadis Platinum (top 1%) and a CDP "A" supplier-engagement rating.
