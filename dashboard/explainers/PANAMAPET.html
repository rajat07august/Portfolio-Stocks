Where a base-oil processor sits

An industry primer for Panama Petrochem: what white oil, petroleum jelly and transformer oil physically are, why 83 paise of every rupee is spent on oil before the plant does anything, how a three-month stockpile and a one-month pricing lag turn a crude shock into a year's profit in one quarter, and why the margin percentage is the wrong number to watch
Built 19-Sep-2026 · Sourced from Panama's FY26 Annual Report, audited and reviewed results (rendered page images), six earnings-call transcripts (May-2022 → Nov-2023), two investor decks (Aug-2022, Nov-2024) and three ICRA rationales · Companion to Company Profiles/PANAMAPET.md

This page explains the industry, not the investment case. It has no valuation, no conviction and no target; those live in the profile. What follows is the mechanism: what the products are, where Panama sits between the refinery and the shampoo bottle, what it actually does to the oil it buys, and why the timing of its purchases, rather than its own work on the oil, is what moves its profit in any given quarter.

1. The products, physically: one oil, eighty grades

Everything Panama sells starts as the same thing: base oil. When a refinery distils crude, the heavy, slow-moving fraction left after petrol, diesel and kerosene have boiled off is cleaned up and sold as base oil. It is a clear-to-yellow liquid that looks like cooking oil and is chemically a mixture of long hydrocarbon chains. Most of the world's base oil becomes engine lubricant. A smaller slice is sold to specialists like Panama, who turn it into oils that go inside things rather than inside engines.

Panama makes "over 80 variants" (ICRA, May-2026; FY26 AR MD&A: "nearly 80"), which fall into six families:

  • White oil (liquid paraffin) and petroleum jelly. Base oil refined until it is colourless, odourless and safe on skin or in the body. This is the oil in baby oil and hair oil, the jelly in a Vaseline-type tub, and a pharmaceutical laxative. ICRA names "Dabur and Marico" among the customers.
  • Rubber process oil. Mixed into rubber compound to make it workable and to extend it. Every tyre contains some.
  • Printing-ink oils. The carrier that holds pigment in offset and newspaper ink.
  • Textile oils (knitting and anti-static coning oils). Sprayed onto yarn so it runs through knitting machines without snapping or building static.
  • Transformer oil. The fluid that fills a power transformer, insulating the windings and carrying the heat away.
  • Others: drilling fluids, cable-filling compounds, specialty greases, waxes and industrial oils.

The point to carry forward is that all six families are the same raw material, finished to a different specification. A white-oil customer and a transformer-oil customer are buying base oil that has been selected, cleaned and tested to meet their particular standard. So the cost of every product moves with one commodity, and it moves at the same time.

2. Where Panama sits between the refinery and the shelf

Panama does not refine crude oil. It buys base oil that a refinery has already made, and sells a finished grade to a company that puts it into a product with a brand on it. It is a middle link with a commodity on one side and a formulator on the other, and it controls the price of neither.

Three things follow from this position.

  • The customer can switch, but not quickly. FMCG and pharma buyers audit the plant and qualify each grade ("Because we have been approved by them, our plans are audited by them," Aug-2022). That makes a supplier sticky. It is not a high barrier, and ICRA calls the industry one of "low entry barriers" where "companies mostly focus on volume, which leads to competitive pricing."
  • The competitor on the specialty grades is often an importer. Management's most consistent claim across all six calls was that it gives multinational formulators "an import substitute at a much more competitive price" (Nov-2022), and that "major competition is from the international players" on the value-added grades.
  • Supply security is part of the product. Half the feedstock sits on formula-linked supply contracts. In normal times that is a procurement detail. When supply is disrupted, it decides who still has oil to sell. "A few of our refinery suppliers have been on maintenance turnaround… However, the company has firm long-term contracts" (May-2023).

3. The ₹100 cost stack: a handling business, not a chemistry business

The FY26 consolidated profit-and-loss account shows exactly where a rupee of Panama's revenue goes. The answer is the most important fact on this page: ₹82.73 of every ₹100 is spent on oil the company bought, before its own work adds anything.

Two things stand out.

  • The value added is thin, and it is mostly logistics and specification. The only process the company itself describes, in three consecutive annual reports and its 2024 deck, is "blending, filling and packaging" and a "technically improved blending system." The FY26 BRSR files the whole business under NIC 19201, "manufacture of refined petroleum products." MY INFERENCE The power-and-fuel line is too small for heavy refining to happen at Panama's plants. The deep refining that makes oil colourless and skin-safe is most likely done upstream by the base-oil supplier, with Panama selecting, finishing, blending and certifying. The company has never described its process, so this cannot be confirmed.
  • Only about ₹17 of every ₹100 responds to Panama's own work. The other ₹83 is oil, bought at a price Panama does not set. When the oil price moves 10%, it moves ₹8.3 of the stack, which is almost as much as the ₹9 of operating profit. That arithmetic is the whole reason the next two sections exist.

4. The clock: three months of stock, one month of pricing lag

If Panama bought oil on Monday and sold it on Tuesday at cost plus a fixed fee, the oil price would pass straight through and the business would earn the same rupee spread whatever crude did. It does not work that way, for three disclosed reasons, and each one puts time between the cost and the price.

Management has described this mechanism in its own words, in the 2022 cycle: "In this quarter, there was an inventory gain as price has moved substantially from the March level" (Aug-2022). ICRA describes the reverse direction: "the one-month lag along with around 50% contracts being fixed-price can result in temporary margin pressures during sharp price movements," and "sharp adverse movements in base oil prices could lead to temporary inventory-related risks."

The fixed-price half cuts the other way. In a rising market, a fixed-price contract keeps the selling price at the old level while the replacement oil gets dearer. That is why, on 15-May-2026, management expected the West Asia disruption to hurt, citing "the lag in price pass-through and the presence of fixed-price contracts." The formula half gains from a rise, and the fixed half loses from it. Which one wins depends on how fast prices move and how long the fixed contracts run, and the second of those is not disclosed. In Q1FY27 the gain won by a very wide margin (§8).

5. A worked example: one tonne, four price paths

The clock is easier to see with numbers. The figure below follows one tonne through four price paths. The numbers are an index, not rupees. Base oil starts at 100, and Panama's rupee spread (its conversion fee, logistics and profit) is held at 21 in every path. That is roughly FY26's 17.3% gross margin.

The four paths show that two separate things move the margin percentage, and neither of them is Panama's skill:

  • The denominator effect (A → B). If base oil settles at a permanently higher price and Panama earns exactly the same rupees per tonne, the percentage margin still falls, because the price it is divided by has doubled. A lower percentage after a price rise is not, by itself, a deterioration.
  • The timing effect (A → C, and C → D). Stock bought before a move is sold after it. This is where windfalls and write-downs come from, and it runs in both directions with the same force.

6. The plants, and the one next to the Gulf refineries

Panama's plants sit in India's western industrial belt, close to the ports that land imported base oil and to the customer clusters: textiles and chemicals in south Gujarat, pharma and FMCG around Mumbai. ICRA notes that the plants are "located strategically to cater to different end-user industries," and that "the company's facility at Taloja is close to the port and is used for most of its exports."

Panol is the part of the company that matters most in a Gulf disruption. It sits beside the West Asian base-oil refineries and loads straight from bulk vessels by pipeline. In normal times that is a freight and working-capital saving. In Q4FY26, ICRA recorded only "limited disruption… marginal impact on utilisation and sales" from the Strait of Hormuz, and operations continued "through alternative routing, continued local supplies and regional dispatches." In Q1FY27, Panol's profit for the quarter (₹91.39 Cr) exceeded its profit for the whole of FY26 (₹69.21 Cr). MY INFERENCE Panol also bought ₹182.6 Cr of traded goods in that one quarter (consolidated ₹185.58 Cr less standalone ₹2.98 Cr), more than in all of FY26. In a supply squeeze, a trader with oil to hand next to the source earns as much from trading as from processing.

7. 🔴 Reading the numbers (I): revenue growth is mostly price

The headline revenue line is the number most likely to mislead a reader of Panama's results. Revenue is tonnes × price, and the price is mostly base oil passing through. The only way to separate the two is revenue per tonne, and volumes were disclosed only while the calls were held.

Three readings follow.

  • A revenue jump with no volume behind it is a price event. FY22 is the template, and Q1FY27 (+150.3% revenue) is a larger version of it. When Panama's revenue moves sharply, the first question is what base oil did, not what Panama did.
  • Rupee profit per tonne is the better gauge, and management declined to use it. An analyst asked on the May-2023 call for "per ton margin", arguing that "your EBITDA margin varies depending upon the oil price going up and going down." Management replied, "We work on the EBITDA percentage." Every margin guidance it gave (13–16%, then 12–15%, 12–14% and 11–13%) was set in percentage terms, the measure that §5 shows moves with the oil price on its own.
  • Profit per tonne roughly tripled from FY18 to FY23 (₹4.2k → ₹13.0k), across the Covid and Russia–Ukraine dislocations, and fell back to ₹9.4k by the last disclosed quarter. MY INFERENCE How much of that is mix improvement and how much is inventory timing cannot be separated without the data the company stopped publishing.

9. The counterweight: the stockpile that earns the gain also eats the cash

The three months of stock that produce a windfall have to be paid for first. Base oil is imported against letters of credit (ICRA: "partial mitigation through LC-backed supplier credit"), it sits in the tank for about a quarter, and customers take around two months to pay ("3-month inventory and 2 months debtors," CFO, Nov-2022). When the price per tonne doubles, the same number of tonnes ties up twice the rupees.

This is the working-capital side of the clock in §4. A processor that holds a quarter of stock is making a bet on the oil price every quarter, whether it means to or not. The bet pays out through gross margin and costs money through working capital. Both sides have to be read together. A year with a large inventory build and negative operating cash flow is not necessarily a bad year, as FY26 shows. It is a year in which the exposure got bigger.

Either the mix went backwards, the per-grade margins compressed, or both, and the company has not said which. The value-added share has not been disclosed since Nov-2023.

Two structural facts sit under the cycle.

  • Volume has not grown. Consolidated volume was ~260,000 t in FY18 and 260,000 t in FY22, with a dip to ~180,000 t in between. FY23 was ~237,000 t by management's own quarterly figures. Capacity went from 240,000 to 270,000 t, and in Aug-2022 management said it was "currently running on 100% capacity utilization at all our units." This is a business whose tonnes grow in steps as plants are added, not continuously.
  • "Specialty" here means spec-matched, not chemically novel. The premium for a value-added grade (roughly 5–6 points of EBITDA margin on management's figures) is real. It is earned by meeting a pharma or FMCG specification reliably, which is exactly the kind of advantage ICRA's "low entry barriers" caveat addresses. The pipeline management named in 2022 (aromatic-free ink distillates, TDAE/RAE rubber oils, biodegradable drilling fluids) moves further along the same spectrum. It does not leave it.

12. What is not disclosed

  • 🔴 Any explanation of Q1FY27. No call, no press release, no deck. The FY26 Annual Report, published 28-Jul-2026, four weeks after the quarter closed, does not address it. Everything in §8 is read from P&L lines.
  • 🔴 Volume, since Q2FY24. The last tonnage disclosed is 65,200 t for the September-2023 quarter. Without it, revenue cannot be split into price and volume, and the denominator effect in §5 cannot be measured.
  • The manufacturing process. The company describes blending, filling and packaging, and nothing about refining, treatment or hydrogenation. Whether Panama's plants do any deep refining, or finish oil that suppliers have already refined, is not stated (§3).
  • Capacity today, plant by plant. The last plant-level figures are from Aug-2022. The consolidated 270,000 t is from Nov-2023. The Ambernath and UAE additions (15,000 t each, as guided) have no confirmed figure.
  • End-market split. Published once, in Aug-2022. The FY26 AR splits sales only into "Panoil" and "Wax". Management's Nov-2023 statement that textile oils were "about 50%" of revenue conflicts with the deck's 19%.
  • The value-added share and its margin. Last given in Nov-2023 (68%). The implied blended margin (~13%) does not reconcile with the reported 9% (§10).
  • Length of the fixed-price contracts, which decides how long the lag in §4 lasts.
  • What Panol trades, and why its revenue share (36%) is more than twice its last-published capacity share (16.7%).
  • Export share, consistently. The standalone geography note gives ₹618.69 Cr of ₹1,961.81 Cr (31.5%) outside India, while the BRSR states exports were 35.69% of turnover. They cannot both be the same measure.
  • The Q1FY27 employee-cost jump (₹8.79 Cr → ₹40.33 Cr consolidated). Unexplained in any filing.

13. Glossary · Sources

Glossary

Base oil
The refined heavy fraction of crude oil, sold by refineries mainly for lubricants. Panama's only real raw material.
Group I / II / III
The industry's classification of base oil by how severely it has been refined: Group I is solvent-refined, while II and III are hydro-processed and purer. Industry background Panama does not say which groups it buys.
White oil / liquid paraffin
Base oil refined until colourless, odourless and chemically inert. Used in cosmetics, pharma and food-contact applications.
Petroleum jelly
A semi-solid blend of white oil and wax: the Vaseline-type product.
Rubber process oil (RPO)
Oil mixed into rubber compound as a softener and extender. TDAE and RAE are low-toxicity versions demanded by tyre makers for regulated markets.
Transformer oil
An insulating, heat-carrying oil that fills power transformers.
Coning / knitting oil
Lubricant applied to yarn so it runs through winding and knitting machines without breaking or building static.
Spread
The rupees per tonne between what the oil cost and what the finished grade sells for. The business's real earning unit.
M-1 pricing
This month's selling price set on last month's base-oil price (ICRA's term). It creates a one-month lag between cost and price.
Formula / fixed-price contract
A formula contract moves with a reference price. A fixed-price contract holds the price for a term. About half of Panama's book is each.
Inventory gain / loss
Profit or loss that comes from selling stock bought at an older price, not from processing it. Produces windfalls in rising markets and write-downs in falling ones.
Denominator effect
A percentage margin falling because the price it is divided by rose, while the rupee spread did not change (§5, path B).
Stock-in-trade
Goods bought for resale without processing: trading, not manufacturing.
EOU
Export-Oriented Unit: a plant licensed mainly for export, with duty benefits. Taloja and the new Ambernath plant are EOUs.
Panol
Panol Industries RMC FZE, Panama's wholly-owned plant in Ras Al Khaimah, UAE.
TPA
Tonnes per annum, a plant's installed capacity.
