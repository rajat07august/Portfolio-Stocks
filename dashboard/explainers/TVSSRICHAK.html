1. What a two-wheeler tyre physically is

A tyre looks like a single moulded object. It is not. It is a laminate — four or five distinct layers of different materials, built up on a drum in sequence and then cooked into one piece under heat and pressure. Understanding the layers is what makes the cost structure, the raw-material exposure and the difference between competitors intelligible.

One detail matters immediately and is specific to this company's category. A motorcycle tyre has a rounded crown; a car tyre has a flat one. A car stays upright, so its tread sits flat on the road. A motorcycle leans — hard — so it needs rubber in contact with the road at forty-five degrees. That single geometric difference means a two-wheeler tyre cannot be made on car-tyre tooling, and it is the physical reason the two categories are separate businesses with separate plants.

Everything downstream follows from one property of this object: the tread wears away and the rest does not. A tyre is the only consumable on a vehicle that is also a structural safety component. Wear is not optional and cannot be deferred forever. That is what creates a replacement market at all — and, as §3 shows, that replacement market is where a tyre maker actually earns its living.

2. Where the materials come from — and why they are 58% of a rupee

A tyre plant is, economically, a converter. It buys world-priced commodities, applies heat, pressure and know-how, and sells a branded object. The value it adds is real but it is thin relative to what passes through, and the company says so in its own risk register: raw materials "represent a significant portion of our selling price." FY26 AR, MD&A §H, Risk 2 — Globalisation Risk, Second Order.

How significant? The FY26 standalone profit and loss account answers precisely. Cost of materials consumed was ₹1,894.55 Cr, purchases of stock-in-trade ₹1.34 Cr, and inventories of finished and work-in-progress goods were drawn down by ₹64.83 Cr — a cost, not a saving. Together, ₹1,960.72 Cr against revenue from operations of ₹3,389.66 Cr = 57.8%. FY26 AR, standalone P&L and Notes 28–29.

The consequence, stated once and then assumed for the rest of this page

With 58% of revenue in commodities and no pricing power over any of them, a tyre maker's margin in any given year is mostly a statement about the gap between when its input costs move and when it can move its own prices. That gap — the pass-through lag — is the single most important mechanism in this industry, and §8 is about how to read it in the numbers. Management's own framing of the position going into FY27 is blunt: "the start to FY27 has seen an unprecedented increase in raw material prices — largely driven by the Middle East conflict." FY26 AR, MD&A §D — Raw Material Trends.

3. The two-sale mechanism: the OEM is the razor, the rider is the blade

A tyre maker sells the identical physical object into two markets that behave nothing like each other.

The first sale is to the vehicle manufacturer. TVS Srichakra supplies two- and three-wheeler OEMs "on Just in Time basis across the length and breadth of the country." FY26 AR, MD&A §C. Getting onto an OEM's approved list takes years of testing and audit, and once there you are rarely displaced — the switching risk for the vehicle maker is a safety recall. But the position is defended annually on price, and the customer is enormous relative to the supplier. The FY26 annual report gives the number that makes this concrete: the top three customers are 39.4% of total revenue — identical to the prior year. FY26 AR, Note 36 — entity-wide disclosures.

The second sale is to the rider whose tread has worn out. It happens three or four years later, through a chain of depots, distributors and retailers, and it is priced by brand and availability rather than by annual negotiation. The Aug-2022 investor presentation — still the only one the company has ever published — sized that network at 38 depots, 500+ distributors and a presence in 480+ districts. Investor Presentation, 10-Aug-2022, slide 14.

Why this is the industry's central fact

A tyre maker's profitability is largely a question of how much of its output goes through the second channel rather than the first. The plant, the tooling and the material cost are identical either way; only the realised price and the cost of reaching the buyer differ. Every strategic move in this industry — brand spending, depot expansion, sponsorship, export distribution — is an attempt to shift the mix. And because the mix is not disclosed, an outside reader cannot measure the one thing that matters most. It has to be inferred from the margin, which is what §7 does.

4. Where TVS Srichakra sits — and where JK Tyre and Balkrishna sit

The natural expectation is that competitors occupy different rungs of a ladder — one upstream, one downstream, one integrated. In tyres that expectation is wrong, and the wrongness is the insight.

Every listed Indian tyre maker occupies the same single step of the value chain. They all buy natural rubber, synthetic rubber, carbon black, cord fabric and bead wire from the same kinds of third-party suppliers. They all mix, build and cure. They all sell through an OEM channel and a replacement channel. None of them is upstream or downstream of any other. They are not arranged vertically at all — they are arranged horizontally, by the category of tyre they choose to make.

So if the chain position is the same, what actually differs?

The category of tyre. And category is not a detail — it sets the ticket size, who the buyer is, how the buyer decides, how much capital a plant needs, and whether the market is domestic or global. Two companies in the same box on Figure 4 can be in businesses with almost nothing in common.

5. The thousand-rupee unit, and what it costs to reach the buyer

The single most clarifying number in this business is the price of one tyre. TVS Srichakra does not publish it — but it publishes enough to bracket it, in two Reg-30 capacity filings and the annual accounts.

Deriving the realisation per tyre MY INFERENCE

Step 1 — installed two- and three-wheeler capacity. Madurai 2W plant: 210–235 lakh tyres per annum. Rudrapur: 92–95 lakh tyres per annum. Total: 302–330 lakh, or 30.2–33.0 million tyres a year. Reg-30 filings, 27-May-2026 and 17-Jan-2026, Annexure 1 in each.

Step 2 — what is actually produced. Stated utilisation is 80–85% at both. That gives 24.2–28.1 million tyres made in a year.

Step 3 — divide revenue by units. FY26 standalone revenue from operations was ₹3,389.66 Cr. If every single rupee of it were two- and three-wheeler tyres, realisation would be ₹1,206 to ₹1,401 per tyre.

Step 4 — correct for the parts that are not. That revenue also contains off-highway tyres and exports, which sell for far more per unit. So the true two-wheeler realisation sits below that range. The average tyre leaves this company for something close to, and probably under, ₹1,200.

One ambiguity, and it only pushes the number down. The Rudrapur filing is not explicit about whether "92 to 95 lakh tyres per annum" is the whole site or Unit-2 alone. If it is Unit-2 alone, total units are higher and the realisation per tyre is lower still. The conclusion is robust to the ambiguity in the only direction it can move.

Now put that thousand-rupee ticket next to what it costs to place the tyre in front of a buyer who has never heard of you. Two lines in the FY26 accounts do that job — Freight Out ₹140.65 Cr, the physical cost of moving tyres to 38 depots and onward, and Advertising and Sales Promotion ₹94.41 Cr, the cost of being the name the rider asks for. FY26 AR, Note 32 — other expenses.

This is the structural handicap of the category, and it is the thing §4 was pointing at. A truck-tyre maker reaches a fleet buyer who orders repeatedly, in quantity, and knows exactly what he is buying. A two-wheeler tyre maker has to reach tens of millions of individuals, one at a time, each spending about a thousand rupees, each advised by a mechanic. The freight network and the brand spend are not discretionary — they are the price of admission. They just happen to cost more than the profit.

7. 🔴 Reading the numbers: FY26's recovery was not mostly raw materials

This is the section that matters most, and it is the one place where the obvious reading of this company's FY26 is wrong.

The received story is that raw materials moderated and the margin recovered. The company itself frames it that way: "The year gone by (FY26) saw a moderation in raw material costs... translating into improved profitability margins." FY26 AR, MD&A §D. Every press note on the result repeated it. And it is not false — materials did ease.

But the P&L says raw materials are only about a third of what happened. Margin before exceptional items and tax went from 1.985% of revenue in FY25 to 3.163% in FY26 — a gain of 1.18 percentage points. Decompose it line by line and the raw-material contribution is 0.42 points. The largest single contributor is not materials at all.

🔴 Why this matters for reading FY27

Employee-cost leverage and finance-cost relief together contributed 0.71 points — more than materials and other income combined. Employee cost barely grew in rupee terms against revenue that grew 12.1%; borrowings fell from ₹812.20 Cr to ₹701.73 Cr, taking the finance line down with them.

Neither of those two levers repeats easily. Operating leverage is a one-time gain on a step-up in volume — at 80–85% utilisation there is not much volume left to lever until the new capacity lands in FY28 (§11). And the ₹430 Cr of approved capex will be funded in part by debt, so the finance line is more likely to rise than fall from here.

MY INFERENCE So the FY26 margin recovery is less repeatable than its own narrative suggests — not because the recovery is fake, but because the parts of it that were largest are the parts that do not recur. And the one lever that could have recurred, raw materials, is the one management has already warned is reversing.

8. Why the raw-material ratio misleads, in both directions

The obvious way to track input cost pressure is raw material as a percentage of revenue. It is the metric the company used itself, in the only investor presentation it has ever published. It is also a metric that cannot distinguish between the two things a reader wants to tell apart.

The ratio falls when selling prices rise faster than input costs, and rises when input costs move first. Input costs always move first, because the tyre maker's own prices reset with a lag — annually with OEMs, and against competitors in the replacement channel. So a falling ratio can mean inputs got cheaper, or it can mean pass-through finally caught up with an old increase. The number alone does not say which.

What to actually watch. Management's warning about FY27 is specific and dated: "the start to FY27 has seen an unprecedented increase in raw material prices — largely driven by the Middle East conflict." FY26 AR, MD&A §D, repeated at §G. The mitigations listed are the standard three — strategic procurement, wastage reduction, and finished-goods price increases. MY INFERENCE Only the third actually defends the margin, and it defends it by giving up volume in a price-sensitive replacement market. The raw-material ratio in the Q2 FY27 result is where that trade-off becomes visible, and it is the first place to look.

9. Ten years: a great deal bigger, a great deal less profitable

The annual report contains a ten-year performance table — a table of numbers, not a chart — and it is the most useful page in the document. Plotted, it says one thing very clearly: this company has spent a decade growing its revenue and losing its margin.

MY INFERENCE That shape is the signature of an industry position, not of a management error. A company whose revenue nearly doubles while its margin halves is one that had to buy its growth on price. In a category where the object costs a thousand rupees and the buyer is advised by a mechanic, that is what growth generally costs — which is precisely the point §4 makes about where this company sits.

10. The export tilt — and the Balkrishna template

There is one part of this business growing fast enough to change the mix, and the accounts identify it precisely. Note 36 splits revenue by geography.

This is what management said would happen, and for once the numbers confirm the sentence: "FY26 saw healthy growth of your Company's global business in both the two & three wheeler space as well as the OHT space, with annual growth of global sales well in excess of growth in domestic sales." FY26 AR, MD&A §A. The country count has moved too — 86 countries in the Aug-2022 deck, about 100 in the FY26 MD&A — and the company is now "placing Company personnel in global markets" rather than relying purely on distributors. MD&A §C.

🔴 The honest counterweight — the one downstream asset it owns is losing money

TVS Srichakra's single move down the value chain (Figure 4) was buying Super Grip Corporation in the USA, acquired 2-Nov-2023 as a channel into the American market. In FY26 it recorded a net operational turnover of ₹91.82 Cr and a loss after tax of ₹18.03 Cr — a −19.6% net margin. FY26 AR, Board's Report, subsidiary performance.

It is being funded by the parent: a further USD 13,50,000 (₹11.92 Cr) loan during FY26, taking total outstanding to USD 55,87,425 (₹52.96 Cr) at 31-Mar-2026. Same source. The MD&A attributes the year to "the tariff situation in the US" and reports "better performance towards the end of FY26."

This is why consolidated profit is lower than standalone profit — ₹71.06 Cr consolidated against ₹82.71 Cr standalone, on ₹255 Cr more revenue. The subsidiaries in aggregate consume earnings. MY INFERENCE Owning distribution abroad is the correct strategic instinct for an exporter, and it is also the hardest part to execute; on the FY26 numbers this particular attempt has destroyed value, and there is no earnings call on which to ask when it breaks even.

How far is the template? The off-highway market management sizes at over US$20 billion is the market Balkrishna built a business of Balkrishna's size inside. TVS Srichakra's entire export line — two-wheeler, three-wheeler and off-highway together — is ₹667.48 Cr. MY INFERENCE The direction of travel is right and the growth rate is real, but the distance is very large, and nothing in the filings suggests management has claimed otherwise. They have not claimed anything; there is no guidance on record.

13. What is not disclosed · Glossary · Sources

What is not disclosed

Stated as gaps, because they are gaps — not inferred away.

  • The OEM versus replacement split. Never disclosed, in any filing, ever. The company reports a single segment: "manufacture and sale of tyres." Note 36. This is the most important number in the business (§3) and it is absent.
  • The two-wheeler versus off-highway revenue split. Also absent, for the same reason. Off-highway is described as a growth pathway and given a ₹110 Cr expansion, but its contribution is never sized.
  • Volumes. No tyre count appears anywhere in the accounts or the MD&A. Realisation per tyre had to be derived from capacity filings (§5) rather than read.
  • The raw-material basket. No mix, no split between natural and synthetic rubber, no import share, no hedging disclosure beyond a statement that the company "imports raw materials from outside India."
  • Market share. The MD&A says the company "sustained market share in the replacement market in the face of intense competition" and has "achieved significant share in the growing E-rickshaw segment" — neither is quantified anywhere.
  • Off-highway capacity in a usable unit. Filed as 75–85 metric tons per annum, which cannot be right (§11).
  • Whether Rudrapur's 92–95 lakh tyres is the whole site or Unit-2 alone. The filing is ambiguous; §5 notes the direction the ambiguity pushes.
  • The ₹18.16 Cr gap between Note 36's geography total (₹3,371.50 Cr) and revenue from operations (₹3,389.66 Cr).
  • Any path to breakeven for Super Grip USA, after two full years of losses and ₹52.96 Cr of parent lending.
  • 🔴 Guidance of any kind, and the forum to ask for it. No revenue target, no margin target, no capex phasing beyond the Reg-30 dates. The company has never held a quarterly earnings call and has published one investor presentation in its history, in August 2022. Every inference on this page exists because a question could not be asked.

Glossary

Bias-ply
Construction in which the carcass cords run diagonally, crossing layer over layer. Most two-wheeler tyres are built this way. Cheaper to tool and to make than a radial.
Radial
Construction in which the carcass cords run straight across from bead to bead, with a separate stiff belt under the tread. Runs cooler and lasts longer; needs far more capital per tonne of output. Universal in passenger cars, and the direction truck tyres have been moving in India for two decades.
Carcass
The structural body of the tyre — the rubberised cord plies that carry the air pressure and the load.
Bead
The hoop of high-tensile steel wire at each inner edge that clamps the tyre onto the wheel rim.
Carbon black
A fine black powder made from heavy oil residue, mixed into rubber to make it hard-wearing. One of the four big cost items in a tyre, and the reason tyres are black.
Calendering
Passing cord fabric between heated rollers to coat it in rubber, producing the sheet the carcass is built from.
Curing
The final step: the built green tyre is pressed into a mould under heat, which vulcanises the rubber into one piece and stamps in the tread pattern. Curing presses are the capital-intensive heart of a tyre plant, which is why utilisation matters so much.
OEM
Original Equipment Manufacturer — the vehicle maker. The first of the two sales (§3).
Replacement market
Also "aftermarket". Sales to the owner of a vehicle already on the road. The second sale, and where the margin is.
Parc
The installed base of vehicles on the road. Replacement demand tracks the parc, not new-vehicle sales — which is what makes it an annuity.
OHT
Off-Highway Tyres — agricultural, construction, industrial and materials-handling tyres. Balkrishna's entire business, and TVS Srichakra's second leg.
TBR / PCR
Truck & Bus Radial / Passenger Car Radial. The two categories that dominate the larger listed Indian tyre makers and that TVS Srichakra is not in.
Pass-through lag
The gap between an input cost moving and the selling price following. In tyres it is the single biggest determinant of any given year's margin (§2, §8).
Just-in-time
Supplying an OEM to its assembly schedule rather than into its warehouse. Deepens the customer relationship and shifts inventory risk onto the supplier.
RODTEP
Remission of Duties or Taxes on Export Products — an Indian export incentive, recognised by the company as government grant income.
