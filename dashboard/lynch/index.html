Start here — how this screen works

Lynch classifies a company before testing it, because what counts as good news depends entirely on what kind of company it is. A low P/E is a bargain in a stalwart and a warning in a cyclical. A missing dividend is fatal in a slow grower and a positive in a fast one.

1

Classify into six categories the first real judgement

C1Slow grower — 2–4% growth. You own it for the dividend. “Why else would you own them?”
C2Stalwart — 10–12%. Bought for a 30–50% gain, then rotated. Recession ballast.
C3Fast grower — 20–25%. “the land of the 10- to 40-baggers.” Where the tenbaggers live, and where the money is lost.
C4Cyclical — earnings that fall as well as rise. “It is here that the unwary stockpicker is most easily parted from his money, and in stocks that he considers safe.”
C5Turnaround — “no growers”, in crisis. Largest absolute gains; least correlated with the market.
C6Asset play — something valuable the market has overlooked. Low risk, high gain.

What this watchlist turned out to be: 36 fast growers, 18 cyclicals, 7 stalwarts, 1 slow grower, 2 turnarounds — and zero asset plays. The category Lynch rates low-risk and high-gain is entirely absent. Every name here is an earnings-growth bet, which is a concentration of method, invisible on a sector chart.

2

Six universal tests 19 excluded

U1Neglect — “the percentage of institutional ownership. The lower the better.”
U2Debt quantum — “companies that have no debt can't go bankrupt.”
U3🔴 Debt structure — is the debt callable on demand? “It's the kind of debt, as much as the actual amount, that separates the winners from the losers in a crisis.”
U4Customer concentration — “25 to 50 percent of its wares to a single customer is a precarious situation.”
U5Diworseification — acquisitions proven one at a time, or a spree?
U6Capital allocation — buybacks and dividends, or cash dissipated?

Every one of the 19 exclusions came from U3 alone. See the chart below — it is the single most destructive test in this screen.

3

Category-specific tests 23 excluded · 6 abstained

Each category is judged on its own terms. The two that did the work here:

C3The 25% ceiling. “I'm wary of companies that seem to be growing faster than 25 percent. Those 50 percenters usually are found in hot industries, and you know what that means.” — six names excluded on this alone, and MACPOWER and CFF now join them
C4🔴 The inverted P/E. “Anticipate a shrinking p/e multiple as business recovers, when peak earnings are achieved.” — a low multiple on peak earnings is a sell signal, not a bargain

ABSTAIN is not a failure. Six names lack the published history to be judged at all. Lynch permits deciding on incomplete information — “the scientific mind that needs to know all the data will be thwarted here” — so these are recorded as my failure to establish the facts, not as a defect of the company.

4

Price 13 excluded

One formula, and it is the book's central one:

(earnings growth rate + dividend yield) ÷ P/E
“Less than a 1 is poor, and 1.5 is okay, but what you're really looking for is a 2 or better.”

With two refusals attached, so the test cannot be misused: “buying a company with mediocre prospects just because the stock is cheap is a losing technique” and “selling an outstanding fast grower because its stock seems slightly overpriced is a losing technique.”

Growth is the 5-year earnings CAGR (10-year where 5 is unavailable). Price ratio is (growth + yield) ÷ P/E. 40 of 64 names have both.

The two halves of the finding.
  • The well-behaved growers are too expensive. Six names sit in Lynch's 15–25% band — ANTHEM, CARYSIL, GARWARE, SANSERA, SHREEPUSHK and JGCHEM — and all six fail the price gate, at 0.19, 0.69, 0.55, 0.34, 0.88 and 0.76.
  • The cheap names are cyclicals at the top. Seven of the nine names clearing the gate are cyclicals whose latest earnings are within 10% of their all-time peak (the other two, ATLANTAELE and CFF, are fast growers past the 35% ceiling; PANAMAPET is the new one, at a 5.9x P/E on windfall earnings). Their ratios look wonderful because the E is temporarily high. A conventional low-P/E screen buys every one of them.
  • The ideal box is not empty — it holds exactly two names, and both are disqualified by what kind of company they are. DEEP is a cyclical at its peak; NLC carries a D/E of 130%. Neither fails on growth or on price.

🔴 Why 19 names died on one test

U3 asks a single question: if a lender got nervous tomorrow, could it demand its money back? Lynch's controlled pair — GCA, carrying $114m of bank debt, “went kaput and was bought out at about 10 cents a share”, while Applied Materials with $17m of debt and $36m of cash went $8 to $36. “The debt burden was the difference.”

Share of total borrowings sitting in current (callable) liabilities, read from each company's FY26 balance sheet. 25 annual reports downloaded and parsed; two hand-verified against the source statements before anything was built on the numbers.

But read this as a finding about India, not about any one company. Indian mid-caps finance themselves on cash credit, packing credit and working-capital demand loans — renewed annually, and in law repayable on demand. Lynch's test is not calibrated for that market, and applying it honestly fails four-fifths of the names that carry debt. The counter-argument deserves stating: in practice these limits are sticky and are rarely pulled from a going concern. But his point is precisely about what happens when a company stops being a comfortable credit — and the frozen criterion excludes on this test alone. It was applied as written rather than softened after the damage was visible.

  • Neither is wrong. They are asking different questions of the same companies.
Against the book — 4 of 6 disclosed holdings excluded, but mostly on price.
  • MAYUR is one of the three survivors. DCMSHRIRAM is the only holding correctly positioned in its cycle — a cyclical trading off-peak.
  • GARWARE (0.53) and EPL (0.57) are excluded on price alone, on businesses the framework otherwise likes. Lynch's own instruction cuts both ways here: “selling an outstanding fast grower because its stock seems slightly overpriced is a losing technique.” This is not a sell signal — only a statement about where he would add.
  • DATAMATICS — growth stalled: 1.8% over three years against 13.4% over ten, which is one of his named sell signs.
  • DEEP is the one worth pausing on. Both frameworks reject it by unrelated routes — Fisher on two consecutive Q4 write-offs, Lynch because an oil-services company at peak earnings on 12.6× is a cyclical at the top, not a cheap stock.

What this screen does not claim

  • U4, U5 and U6 are incomplete. Three customer-concentration failures were established on evidence — SSWL's Hyundai at 74%, Concord's Indian Railways, Rajesh's Gujarat utilities — and one pass. The rest is IE, so no survivor has been cleared on customer concentration.
  • The rollout test and the one-shot fragility test were never reached for most fast growers, because the growth band or the price gate excluded them first. Those names are excluded on what was tested.
  • Insider buying is IE throughout, as pre-registered — Indian disclosure gives aggregate promoter holding, not the “seven vice presidents buying 1,000 shares apiece” granularity Lynch wants.
  • 🔴 Blinding was imperfect, and more so than in the Fisher run — the holdings list was already in context, and the Fisher result was known before this screen ran, a contamination Fisher did not carry. The mitigation is that the criteria were frozen first and the decisive tests are arithmetic. It is a real limitation.
  • 🔴 Three parse failures were caught mid-run and two changed a verdict. A first extractor returned KMEW at −24,444,108. A second captured note references as values and wrongly excluded DCMSHRIRAM. Plotting the data for this page exposed a third: NLC was in the wrong category, sitting inside the ideal zone where no slow grower can be. A parse that looks right is not a parse that has been checked — and a chart catches what a table hides.
